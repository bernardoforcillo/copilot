# Behavioral economics & choice architecture -> design

Behavioral economics studies the systematic, predictable ways people deviate from rational-actor models when facing choices under risk, time, and effort — the same biases and heuristics are the raw material of both ethical choice architecture and manipulative dark patterns, so this file pairs every mechanism with its evidence and its ethical boundary.

## Prospect Theory, Loss Aversion & Its Limits (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Gal & Rucker, 2018)
**Field & mechanism:** Prospect theory replaced expected-utility theory as the descriptive model of choice under risk. People evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a *reference point* (not final wealth states); the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses (risk-averse when ahead, risk-seeking when behind), and — the headline claim — losses are weighted more heavily than equivalent gains ("loss aversion"), with early estimates putting the loss/gain weighting ratio around 2–2.25. A companion probability-weighting function overweights small probabilities and underweights large/near-certain ones, explaining both lottery-ticket buying and insurance purchase.
**Evidence:** Kahneman & Tversky's original hypothetical-choice studies with Israeli and Swedish samples spawned one of the most cited papers in economics and a Nobel Prize (Kahneman, 2002). But Gal & Rucker's 2018 review of the loss-aversion literature specifically (not prospect theory as a whole) found the "losses loom larger" asymmetry is far less general than textbooks suggest: many choice, pricing, and matching studies fail to show greater sensitivity to losses than gains, and the effect appears strongly moderated by elicitation method (choice vs. matching tasks), domain (money vs. time vs. effort vs. consumer goods), and how cleanly the reference point is defined.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: emotion/motivation)* Reference-dependence itself is robust and design-relevant even where the loss>gain asymmetry is contested — framing retention or upgrade moments around what a user already has ("you'll lose access to your saved projects") versus a symmetric future-gain frame ("get access to more projects") can shift behavior; streaks and progress bars work by making the current state feel like an asset to protect.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Because Gal & Rucker found the core "loss aversion is universal and larger than gain-seeking" claim unreliable outside specific setups, do not treat loss-framed copy as an automatic, universal conversion lever — validate per context. And manufacturing artificial losses (fake "you're about to lose this deal" countdowns) to trigger the bias is an ethical bright line, not a design pattern.
**Cite:** [BE-01] [BE-02]

## Anchoring & Adjustment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974)
**Field & mechanism:** Among the heuristics catalogued in "Judgment under Uncertainty," anchoring-and-adjustment describes how people estimate unknown quantities by starting from an available initial value — even an arbitrary or irrelevant one — and adjusting away from it insufficiently, so the final judgment stays biased toward the anchor.
**Evidence:** The classic demonstration spun a rigged wheel-of-fortune to land on 10 or 65, then asked participants to estimate the percentage of African countries in the UN; median estimates were 25% and 45% respectively — a large shift from a number participants knew was random. The effect has since been replicated across price estimation, legal sentencing recommendations, and real-estate and negotiation contexts.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: attention/perception)* The first number a user sees anchors every subsequent numeric judgment: a crossed-out "was" price, a suggested tip percentage, the default position of a price slider, or which pricing tier is shown first (highest-first anchors perceived value upward for the tiers that follow).
**Where the analogy breaks:** Anchoring on a fabricated or inflated reference number (fake "original" prices, invented MSRPs) is both an ethical violation and, in many jurisdictions (FTC guidance in the US, pricing-transparency rules in the EU), a legally actionable deceptive practice — anchors must be truthful.
**Cite:** [BE-03]

## Framing Effects (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981)
**Field & mechanism:** Logically and numerically equivalent descriptions of the same option produce systematically different choices depending on whether the description is framed as a gain or a loss — the reference point set by the *wording*, not just the outcome, drives risk attitude.
**Evidence:** The canonical "Asian disease" experiment framed an identical policy outcome as either lives *saved* or lives *lost*; participants were risk-averse under the "saved" (gain) frame and risk-seeking under the "lost" (loss) frame, despite the underlying probabilities being identical. This directional pattern has replicated widely across medical decision-making, policy-support surveys, and consumer choice, though the size of the shift varies by domain.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: emotion/cognitive load)* Identical facts land differently depending on frame — "95% of users keep this default on" reads very differently from "5% turn this off" even though they describe the same population; error states framed as recoverable ("we couldn't save — try again") rather than as failure reduce user anxiety without changing the underlying facts.
**Where the analogy breaks:** A frame is ethically legitimate only when it is factually equivalent to its alternative and the choice remains genuinely free; selecting whichever frame produces the outcome the business wants, regardless of what best informs the user, is where framing crosses into the manipulation covered in the dark-patterns section below.
**Cite:** [BE-04]

## Defaults, Status-Quo Bias & Choice Architecture (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008; Johnson & Goldstein, 2003; Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988; Jachimowicz et al., 2019)
**Field & mechanism:** Thaler & Sunstein's *Nudge* coined "choice architecture" — the observation that how options are presented (order, defaults, framing) shapes decisions without removing options or changing incentives. Its most powerful single tool is the default: because switching away from the status quo carries effort cost, feels like a potential loss, and can be read as an implied recommendation, people disproportionately stick with whatever is pre-selected (Samuelson & Zeckhauser's "status-quo bias").
**Evidence:** Johnson & Goldstein's organ-donation study is the field's most cited demonstration: countries using opt-out (presumed-consent) donor registration averaged roughly 98% effective consent, versus roughly 15% in opt-in countries — a default-driven gap of tens of percentage points on a life-and-death decision. Jachimowicz et al.'s 2019 meta-analysis of default-effect studies found a robust average effect (Cohen's d ≈ 0.68 across ~58 studies) that is not uniform — it is largest when the decision is complex, effortful to reverse, or ambiguous, and smaller when the choice is simple or high-stakes enough that people deliberate regardless.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: motor/cognitive load — reduces the required action to zero)* Pre-select the option that is safest, most common, or genuinely best for most users — privacy-protective settings, the recommended plan, accessible font sizes — rather than defaulting to whatever benefits the business most.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The same lever that lifts retirement-plan enrollment or organ donation can default users into data-sharing, marketing consent, or auto-renewing subscriptions they would not actively choose; this is precisely why GDPR and similar regulations mandate opt-in (not opt-out) for many forms of consent — when a default serves the choice architect over the user, it has become sludge, not a nudge.
**Cite:** [BE-05] [BE-06] [BE-07] [BE-08]

## The Decoy Effect / Asymmetric Dominance (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982; Frederick, Lee & Baskin, 2014)
**Field & mechanism:** Adding a third option to a choice set that is strictly worse than one existing option on every attribute (but not strictly worse than the other) increases the chosen share of the option that dominates it — the "attraction effect." This violates the rational-choice regularity axiom, which says adding an option should never increase an existing option's share.
**Evidence:** Huber, Payne & Puto demonstrated the effect across product categories (beer, cars, restaurants) in incentive-compatible lab studies. Frederick, Lee & Baskin's 2014 direct-replication program is an important corrective: across a large series of studies using real products and incentivized (not merely hypothetical) choices, the classic attraction effect was substantially weaker and less reliable than the original textbook framing implies, and frequently failed to replicate outright under those stricter conditions.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: perception/attention — comparison is relative, not absolute)* A three-tier pricing page where the middle "target" plan is flanked by a tier that is strictly inferior on the attributes shown nudges attention and choice toward the target; comparison tables that let a recommended plan visibly dominate a shown alternative use the same mechanism.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Given Frederick & Baskin's findings, do not assume the classic decoy effect will appear in your funnel — test it. And a decoy tier engineered purely to be un-chosen (a "junk" plan nobody should buy) rather than to genuinely aid comparison erodes trust once users notice it exists only to manipulate.
**Cite:** [BE-09] [BE-10]

## Choice Overload — and Its Limits (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, 2010)
**Field & mechanism:** The proposal that more options can *reduce* motivation to choose at all, because comparing and evaluating a larger assortment raises cognitive effort and anticipated regret.
**Evidence:** Iyengar & Lepper's famous jam-tasting study found a 24-jam display attracted more browsers than a 6-jam display, but the 6-jam display converted to purchase roughly ten times more often (about 30% versus 3%) — and follow-up studies with chocolates and extra-credit essay topics showed the same direction. However, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd's 2010 meta-analysis of about 50 published and unpublished choice-overload experiments found the *average* effect across the literature is close to zero with very high heterogeneity — the effect reverses or vanishes under many common conditions (clear pre-existing preferences, easy-to-compare options, time pressure), meaning choice overload is real in some contexts and absent or reversed in most others.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: cognitive load — Hick's-law adjacent)* Default long catalogs or settings screens to a curated subset (a "Recommended" view of 3–5 items) with progressive disclosure to "see all," and offer filters that let users narrow large assortments rather than removing them.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Given the null meta-analytic result, blanket option-hiding is not justified by "choice overload" alone — expert users, collectors, and high-involvement purchases often want and benefit from full assortments. Artificially restricting real options to funnel users toward a specific (e.g., higher-margin) SKU is a materially different and more ethically fraught move than genuine complexity reduction.
**Cite:** [BE-11] [BE-12]

## Hyperbolic Discounting (Laibson, 1997)
**Field & mechanism:** People discount the near future far more steeply than the distant future, formalized as quasi-hyperbolic (β-δ) discounting: a delay from "today" to "tomorrow" is weighted very differently than an equal one-day delay far in the future (e.g., day 30 vs. day 31). This produces time-inconsistent preferences and present-biased impulsivity — procrastination, undersaving, and choices a person's own "future self" would not have made.
**Evidence:** Laibson's model formalized and extended earlier experimental discounting patterns showing implied discount rates are far higher over short horizons than long ones, and it underlies the later empirical literature on commitment devices and self-control (see BEX-08/09 below).
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: motivation/motor — immediacy of feedback)* Make delayed-but-beneficial value visible now — progress visualizations, small immediate rewards tied to long-term goals (a savings app celebrating today's small deposit) — and reduce friction on future-oriented actions (auto-enroll, scheduled transfers) since users systematically undervalue effort spent now for a future payoff.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The same present-bias mechanism that helps build good habits is exploited by manufactured urgency ("only 2 left," countdown timers) to trigger impulsive purchases the user would not endorse on reflection — designers should distinguish helping a user act on their *own* stated long-term goal now from manufacturing false urgency to bypass their reflection entirely.
**Cite:** [BE-13]

## Dark patterns & sludge
**Field & mechanism:** "Dark patterns" (Harry Brignull's term) are interface designs that deploy the exact mechanisms catalogued above — loss aversion, defaults, framing, decoys, urgency — to benefit the business at the user's expense: confirm-shaming, hidden costs revealed only at checkout, forced continuity, and "roach motel" flows that are easy to enter and hard to leave. Thaler's "sludge" (2018) names the mirror image of a nudge: friction deliberately added to discourage an action that would benefit the user (a maze of steps to cancel a subscription) rather than reduced to help them.
**Evidence:** Mathur et al.'s 2019 automated crawl of roughly 11,000 shopping websites found dark patterns on about 11% of sites, cataloguing 15 distinct types across 7 categories — false urgency countdown timers and confirmshaming were the most common. Luguri & Strahilevitz's 2021 controlled experiment (N > 1,900) manipulated the aggressiveness of dark patterns in a sign-up flow and found "aggressive" dark patterns roughly tripled sign-up rates versus a neutral design (about 21% baseline conversion versus roughly 62% with aggressive dark patterns), with less sophisticated and more vulnerable users disproportionately affected; even "mild" dark patterns produced measurable, smaller lifts.
**Transfer to design:** Apply a symmetry principle — a cancellation flow should take no more steps than the sign-up flow it mirrors; disclosures (recurring cost, data use) should be at least as prominent as the enticement; countdown timers and scarcity claims should be used only when literally, verifiably true.
**Where the analogy breaks:** This section *is* the boundary condition for nearly every mechanism above. Regulatory exposure is concrete and growing: FTC enforcement actions specifically target dark patterns in the US, the EU's Digital Services Act and Unfair Commercial Practices framework prohibit manipulative interface design, and US state laws (e.g., California's dark-pattern provisions under the CCPA/CPRA) specifically target confirmshaming and forced continuity in cancellation flows.
**Cite:** [BE-14] [BE-15] [BE-16]

## Do Nudges Actually Work? A Live Controversy (Mertens et al., 2022; Maier et al., 2022)
**Field & mechanism:** Two meta-analyses of overlapping nudge literatures reach opposite headline conclusions, and the contrast is itself the design-relevant finding. Mertens, Herberz, Hahnel & Brosch (2022) aggregated 447 effect sizes from 212 studies and reported a moderate-to-large average nudge effect (Cohen's d ≈ 0.45), with default, framing, and social-norm nudges strongest. Maier et al. (2022) reanalyzed nudge studies applying corrections for publication bias (selection models, PET-PEESE) and found the corrected average effect shrinks toward zero and is not reliably distinguishable from no effect — arguing the field's apparent effectiveness is substantially inflated by publication bias and underpowered studies.
**Evidence:** Both are large-scale, methodologically serious meta-analyses of essentially the same underlying literature; the disagreement turns on how aggressively each corrects for publication bias, and it remains an unresolved, actively debated methodological controversy in behavioral economics.
**Transfer to design:** Treat any single "nudge X increases conversion by Y%" claim — from a paper, a vendor, or a case study — as a hypothesis to validate in your own product via A/B testing, not as a guaranteed lever; prioritize mechanisms with clear causal logic and large, pre-registered evidence over cute-sounding lab demonstrations.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Given Maier et al.'s correction, be skeptical of "nudge theater" — small UI tweaks marketed as behavior-change silver bullets with citations to the pre-correction literature — and measure real effect in your own context rather than citing the literature as settled science.
**Cite:** [BE-17] [BE-18]

## Design checklist
- Frame retention, safety, and upgrade moments around genuine stakes (what the user actually has or actually risks) — not manufactured losses — and A/B test loss- vs. gain-framed copy per context rather than assuming loss framing always wins.
- Anchor prices and estimates only with truthful reference numbers; never fabricate "was" prices or inflated comparisons.
- Default users into the option that is safest or most broadly beneficial for them, not the option that benefits the business most; keep consent for data sharing and marketing opt-in, not opt-out.
- Reduce a large catalog or settings surface to a curated "Recommended" view with progressive disclosure, but let power users reach the full set — don't hide options solely to manufacture false scarcity.
- If you use a comparison/decoy tier, make it a genuinely reasonable (if inferior) option, not a "junk" tier that exists only to be rejected — and validate the effect in your own funnel rather than assuming the textbook result.
- Apply a symmetry rule: any flow that is easy to enter (sign-up, subscribe, opt in) must be equally easy to exit (cancel, unsubscribe, opt out).
- Use urgency and scarcity messaging only when literally, verifiably true; treat countdown timers and "X left" claims as regulatory and trust risk when fabricated.
- Cite the specific mechanism and its boundary condition when defending a design decision in review — "we use a default here because Johnson & Goldstein / Jachimowicz et al., but we kept it opt-in because the consent is sensitive" is the auditable form this checklist should produce.

## Deeper dive (v3)

## Mental Accounting (Thaler, 1985, 1999)
**Field & mechanism:** People do not treat money as fungible. Instead they partition wealth into separate mental "accounts" (rent money, vacation fund, a tax refund treated as "found money") and evaluate purchases using "transaction utility" — perceived deal quality relative to a reference price — in addition to ordinary "acquisition utility" from the good itself. People also apply hedonic-framing rules to how gains and losses are grouped: segregating multiple gains (announce them separately) and integrating multiple losses (announce them together) maximizes subjective net pleasure even when the totals are identical.
**Evidence:** Thaler's synthesis includes now-classic vignette studies: people are far less willing to buy a replacement theater ticket after losing a $X ticket already paid for than to spend an equivalent $X in lost cash on the same ticket, because the ticket purchase draws on the same "entertainment account" while the lost cash does not. Similarly, people report being willing to drive 20 minutes to save $5 on a $15 calculator but not to save the same $5 on a $125 jacket — identical absolute savings, different relative framing within the "purchase account."
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: cognitive load/framing)* Itemized savings ("you saved $40 this month") tend to land better than an aggregated total, because savings sit in a mentally separate account from spending; budgeting apps that create named sub-accounts/"envelopes" directly leverage this; per-day pricing ("just $0.33/day") reframes an annual cost against a smaller, more comfortable reference account.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The same reframing that clarifies genuine value can be used to obscure true cost — per-day pricing on a large non-refundable annual charge is designed specifically to minimize the salience of the real one-time debit. Legitimate use clarifies value against an honest reference point; manipulative use hides the total the user is actually committing to.
**Cite:** [BEX-01] [BEX-02]

## The Endowment Effect — and a Major Critique (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990, 1991; Plott & Zeiler, 2005)
**Field & mechanism:** Merely owning or possessing an object increases its subjective value, producing a gap between willingness-to-accept (WTA, the price to give something up) and willingness-to-pay (WTP, the price to acquire the same thing) — typically explained as loss aversion applied to a reference point of current possession, so giving up an owned good is coded as a loss, weighted more heavily than the equivalent gain of acquiring it.
**Evidence:** Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler's "mug experiments" randomly gave half of a group a coffee mug, then had owners set a selling price and non-owners set a buying price; sellers demanded roughly double what buyers offered, replicated across many goods and market structures, and cited as a real-world violation of the Coase theorem's prediction that initial allocation shouldn't matter when trade is costless. Plott & Zeiler's 2005 methodological critique found that when the elicitation procedure (Becker-DeGroot-Marschak mechanism) is taught thoroughly and potential subject misconceptions about it are removed, the WTA-WTP gap shrinks dramatically or disappears — suggesting a substantial share of the "classic" effect in less careful studies may be a procedural artifact rather than a pure preference phenomenon.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: motor/emotion — ownership framing)* Free trials, "your cart," pre-purchase customization ("build your own"), and try-before-you-buy flows increase felt ownership and thus willingness to keep or pay for the item; showing a user's saved configuration as already "theirs" leverages the same mechanism.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Per Plott & Zeiler, the endowment effect is not the robust, universal law that popular design writing often assumes — it is sensitive to elicitation format, incentive structure, and subject understanding. Do not treat "make them feel like they own it" as a guaranteed conversion lever, and never use pre-checked "your item is reserved" language to manufacture *false* endowment or urgency — that is a dark pattern, not an application of the research.
**Cite:** [BEX-03] [BEX-04] [BEX-05]

## Sunk Cost Fallacy (Arkes & Blumer, 1985)
**Field & mechanism:** People show a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once money, effort, or time has already been invested in it — even when continuing has negative expected value going forward — because abandoning the endeavor forces the person to consciously realize the sunk investment as "wasted."
**Evidence:** Arkes & Blumer's field studies include a theater-subscription price manipulation: patrons who paid full price for a season subscription attended significantly more performances over the following season than patrons who received the same subscription at a discount, even though the price paid was sunk either way by the time attendance decisions were made. Roth, Robbert & Straus's 2015 meta-analysis confirmed a reliable, moderate overall sunk-cost effect across the literature, moderated by decision domain (larger in some financial/investment contexts), whether the investment was self-made versus inherited, and whether the original commitment was public (larger when publicly visible, consistent with a self-justification/consistency account alongside the pure loss-avoidance account).
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: motivation — investment/progress framing)* Onboarding flows and "profile completion" progress indicators legitimately capitalize on invested-effort motivation to finish a beneficial setup process; multi-step forms that visibly show accumulated progress encourage completion of genuinely useful tasks.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Exploiting sunk cost to keep users trapped in a poor-fit product or subscription ("you've already put in 6 months of data — don't lose it") rather than helping them make the objectively right call for themselves is manipulative. Ethical use nudges completion of things that are genuinely good for the user; it does not use accumulated investment as leverage against a decision that no longer serves them.
**Cite:** [BEX-06] [BEX-07]

## Present Bias & Self-Control (O'Donoghue & Rabin, 1999; DellaVigna & Malmendier, 2006)
**Field & mechanism:** O'Donoghue & Rabin formalized present-biased (quasi-hyperbolic) preferences applied specifically to self-control problems, and distinguished "sophisticated" agents — who anticipate their own future present bias and adopt commitment devices — from "naive" agents, who do not anticipate it and repeatedly, predictably procrastinate. DellaVigna & Malmendier's field study is the landmark empirical test of this in a real market: gym-goers on flat monthly contracts.
**Evidence:** Analyzing roughly 7,752 members across three US health clubs, DellaVigna & Malmendier found average monthly-contract members paid about $70/month while attending fewer than 5 times per month on average — more than double the effective per-visit cost of an available $10 pay-per-visit alternative — and many members who eventually cancelled kept paying for months after their last actual gym visit. The pattern is best explained by present bias interacting with over-optimistic beliefs about one's own future attendance, not by rational insurance-like reasoning about attendance uncertainty.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: motivation/motor)* Support commitment devices — scheduling a future action now, pre-committing to a plan, "future you" framing in reminders — and build in reminders or usage summaries that counteract naive present bias rather than relying on it.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Subscription businesses have a direct commercial incentive to exploit present bias and imperfect self-forecasting rather than correct for it — DellaVigna & Malmendier's finding is literally the profit mechanism many auto-renewing services depend on. Ethical design surfaces actual usage transparently and helps users right-size or cancel a plan that no longer fits, rather than relying on this bias to sustain revenue.
**Cite:** [BEX-08] [BEX-09]

## Nudging vs. Boosting (Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff, 2017)
**Field & mechanism:** Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff contrast two families of behavioral intervention. Nudges (per Thaler & Sunstein) work by exploiting existing cognitive biases and heuristics to steer behavior while formally preserving choice; they typically require no new competence from the person and their effect often disappears once the nudge itself is removed. Boosts instead aim to improve people's own decision-making competences — statistical/risk literacy, transparent decision aids, better-formatted information (e.g., natural-frequency risk formats) — so the person can make better decisions autonomously, including in situations the designer never anticipated, with effects intended to persist without the intervention present.
**Evidence:** The paper is primarily a conceptual/taxonomic framework rather than a single new experiment, but it synthesizes prior boosting evidence — for example, risk-literacy interventions using natural-frequency formats that measurably improve lay Bayesian reasoning — drawing on the broader "fast and frugal heuristics" research program (Gigerenzer and colleagues) on training simple, transferable decision rules.
**Transfer to design:** *(lens: cognitive load — teaching vs. steering)* A nudge auto-selects the "recommended" setting for the user; a boost instead helps the user understand what the setting does and when they would want to change it — transparent explanations, "why we suggest this" affordances, and decision-support visualizations are boosts, and they build durable user competence rather than one-off compliance.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Boosting is more effortful to design, slower to show measurable behavior change, and not always feasible — a time-critical safety decision may genuinely need steering in the moment rather than an in-context lesson in probability. The nudge/boost distinction is itself a design-ethics tool: prefer boosts when the goal is genuinely serving user autonomy and competence, and reserve nudges — bounded by the ethics guardrails established throughout this file — for cases where boosting isn't practical.
**Cite:** [BEX-10]
