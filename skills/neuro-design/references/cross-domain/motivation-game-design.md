# Motivation science & game design -> design

Games are the most extensively studied engagement medium we have: decades of psychology, behavioral economics, and human-computer interaction research on why people keep playing, keep returning, and sometimes keep playing past the point of enjoyment. That last clause is the reason this file exists. The same mechanisms that make a well-designed onboarding flow, habit-forming productivity tool, or learning app genuinely rewarding — clear goals, a sense of progress, well-timed feedback, the satisfaction of getting better at something — are, at high enough dosage and cynicism, the mechanisms behind slot machines and loot boxes. Motivation science does not draw a bright line between "engaging" and "addictive"; it describes a continuum of reinforcement intensity and need-satisfaction, and it is the designer's job to decide where on that continuum a given feature should sit and to be honest with themselves about which side of it they are building on.

This file treats engagement as a legitimate design goal — flow, mastery, and autonomy support are good for users, not just for retention metrics — while flagging, at every mechanism, where the same lever can be turned into a compulsion loop. Read the `## Ethics note` and `## Design checklist` sections as load-bearing, not optional coda.

## Flow: challenge-skill balance and optimal experience

**Field & mechanism:** Positive psychology's account of "optimal experience" — a state of complete absorption in an activity, in which action and awareness merge, self-consciousness drops away, and time perception distorts. Csikszentmihalyi's original interview and experience-sampling studies (of rock climbers, chess players, surgeons, and assembly-line workers alike) located flow at the intersection of high perceived challenge and high perceived skill: too much challenge relative to skill produces anxiety, too little produces boredom, and the flow channel sits between them, widening as skill grows.
**Evidence:** Cross-domain experience-sampling and interview data show the challenge-skill balance point recurring across wildly different activities and cultures, alongside a consistent cluster of preconditions: clear proximal goals, immediate and unambiguous feedback, and a sense of control over the outcome. Sweetser and Wyeth operationalized the construct for games specifically, translating it into an eight-element evaluative model (concentration, challenge, player skill, control, clear goals, feedback, immersion, social interaction) used as a heuristic checklist for game design and playtesting.
**Transfer to design:** Calibrate difficulty/complexity to the user's demonstrated skill and let it ramp as competence grows — this is the design logic behind progressive disclosure, adaptive tutorials, and difficulty curves. Make goals legible at every step (what am I doing, why) and make feedback on action outcomes immediate, so the user's model of "am I succeeding" never lags their action.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Flow is a phenomenological, self-report construct measured mostly by interview and experience-sampling, not a directly observable brain state with a clean physiological signature — treat "flow" as a useful design heuristic, not a measurable KPI. It also describes voluntary, intrinsically chosen activity; manufacturing a flow-like grind in a task the user did not choose (mandatory dashboards, forced daily quotas) borrows the vocabulary without the voluntariness that makes flow experientially positive.
**Cite:** [GM-01], [GM-02]

## Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness

**Field & mechanism:** Self-determination theory (SDT) proposes three basic psychological needs whose satisfaction predicts intrinsic motivation, well-being, and sustained engagement: autonomy (feeling one's actions are self-endorsed, not controlled), competence (feeling effective and capable of growth), and relatedness (feeling connected to others). SDT further distinguishes intrinsic motivation (doing something because it is inherently interesting) from several qualitatively different forms of extrinsic motivation, ranging from externally controlled to fully internalized.
**Evidence:** Deci and Ryan's synthesis draws on decades of laboratory and field studies establishing that need satisfaction, not reward magnitude, is the strongest predictor of sustained, high-quality engagement. Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski applied the framework directly to video games across multiple studies, finding that in-game satisfaction of autonomy and competence (and, in multiplayer contexts, relatedness) predicted game enjoyment, preference for a game, and even short-term changes in well-being after play — independent of a game's violent content or graphical intensity, which had been the field's prior focus.
**Transfer to design:** Give users real choices with consequence (autonomy), visible evidence of growing skill or mastery — levels, unlocked capability, competence feedback rather than empty praise (competence), and, where the product is social, genuine channels for connection rather than performative ones (relatedness). Menus of cosmetic-only "choices" or forced onboarding flows dressed up as options do not satisfy autonomy; they are autonomy's costume.
**Transfer to design:** Design competence signals that are earned and specific ("you cleared this in half the average time") rather than generic and unconditional ("great job!") — generic praise satisfies neither the competence need nor intrinsic motivation research's evidence on informational versus controlling feedback.
**Where the analogy breaks:** SDT describes needs that generalize across domains and cultures, but the *relative weight* of autonomy versus competence versus relatedness varies by person, context, and (per later cross-cultural SDT work not covered in the core citations here) culture — a relatedness-heavy feature (leaderboards, social sharing) can satisfy some users and alienate others who came for solitary mastery.
**Cite:** [GM-03], [GM-04]

## The overjustification effect: when rewards backfire

**Field & mechanism:** Adding an extrinsic reward for an activity someone already finds intrinsically interesting can *reduce* their subsequent interest in doing it unrewarded — the reward re-labels the activity in the person's mind from "something I do because I enjoy it" to "something I do for the reward," and once the reward is removed, the original enjoyment does not fully return.
**Evidence:** Deci's original Soma-cube laboratory experiment found participants paid for solving puzzles spent less time on the puzzles during a subsequent unpaid free-choice period than unpaid participants. Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett's "draw-a-picture" study with preschoolers replicated the effect outside the lab and outside adults: children who expected a reward for an activity (drawing) they already liked showed less free-play interest afterward than children who received no reward or an unexpected one — the study that coined the term "overjustification effect." Deci, Koestner, and Ryan's meta-analysis of 128 studies confirmed the effect is real and robust for tangible, expected, task-contingent rewards on tasks that were interesting to begin with, while verbal praise and positive, informational feedback tend to enhance rather than undermine intrinsic motivation — a nuance often dropped in pop-science summaries of the effect.
**Transfer to design:** Be careful layering points, badges, or extrinsic incentives onto features users already find inherently rewarding (creative tools, exploration, mastery-driven activities) — the incentive can crowd out the very engagement it was meant to boost, especially if it is framed as controlling ("do this to earn X") rather than informational ("you're getting faster at this"). Reserve extrinsic reward mechanics for activities that are not already intrinsically motivating (tedious but necessary onboarding steps, compliance tasks) where there is less to crowd out.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The effect is most reliable for tasks with clear pre-existing intrinsic interest and for tangible, expected, contingent rewards — it does not mean all incentives always backfire; well-designed, informational, non-controlling rewards on low-interest tasks reliably help rather than hurt, per the same meta-analysis.
**Cite:** [GM-05], [GM-06], [GM-07]

## Variable-ratio reinforcement: the psychology of the "one more try"

**Field & mechanism:** Operant conditioning describes how the *schedule* on which a reward follows a behavior shapes response rate and persistence independent of total reward received. Among the four classic schedules (fixed/variable × ratio/interval), variable-ratio schedules — where reward follows an unpredictable number of responses — produce the highest, steadiest response rates and, critically, the greatest resistance to extinction: behavior reinforced on a variable-ratio schedule persists longest after reinforcement stops entirely.
**Evidence:** Ferster and Skinner's systematic laboratory work mapping schedules of reinforcement in animals established this ordering empirically and it has replicated across species and response types since. This is also the mechanism underlying dopaminergic reward-prediction-error signaling described by neuroscientists such as Wolfram Schultz: unpredictable rewards generate larger phasic dopamine responses than predictable ones of the same magnitude, which is part of why variable-ratio schedules are so behaviorally sticky — the nervous system is tuned to value the uncertainty itself.
**Transfer to design:** Unpredictable positive feedback (a surprising discovery, an unexpectedly generous result, a delightful easter egg) can make discovery-driven or exploratory experiences more memorable and engaging than predictable ones — this is legitimate delight design. Notification badges, refresh mechanics, and reward reveals that are deliberately randomized to maximize repeat-checking behavior are the same lever pulled toward compulsion rather than delight.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Operant schedule research was conducted on animals working for survival-relevant reinforcers (food, water) under scarcity — human engagement with software involves symbolic, socially and cognitively mediated rewards, self-awareness of the mechanism, and (usually) freedom to stop; the schedule effect transfers, but the ethical stakes and the user's capacity for informed disengagement do not transfer identically from a Skinner box.
**Cite:** [GM-08], [GM-09]

## The near-miss effect: losses that feel like wins

**Field & mechanism:** In gambling, a "near-miss" — an outcome that falls just short of a win (two matching symbols and a third one position off on a slot machine) — is objectively a loss but is processed by the brain's reward circuitry more like a win than an ordinary loss, and it measurably increases motivation to keep playing.
**Evidence:** Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones, and Gray used fMRI to show that near-miss outcomes recruit reward-related circuitry (including ventral striatum and related regions) similarly to actual wins, despite delivering no payout, and that near-misses specifically enhanced self-reported motivation to continue gambling relative to full misses.
**Transfer to design:** Understand this mechanism primarily as a warning label, not a toolkit item. If a product surfaces "almost" outcomes (score just short of a threshold, streak broken by one day, progress bar stopping at 99%), recognize that this framing is not neutral information design — it is a motivational lever with a well-documented history in gambling design, and deploying it deliberately to extend engagement sessions is functionally the same move.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Not every near-completion signal is manipulative — telling a user honestly that they were one point from a goal can be legitimate, motivating feedback in a skill-based context (a game score, a fitness target) where the near-miss reflects genuine performance. The line is intent and randomness: near-misses engineered into a randomized-outcome system (loot mechanics, randomized rewards) to manufacture false near-success are categorically different from an honest report of a skill-based shortfall.
**Cite:** [GM-10]

## The goal-gradient effect and endowed progress

**Field & mechanism:** Motivation to complete a goal intensifies as perceived distance to that goal shrinks — effort and speed increase disproportionately near the finish line, not linearly across the whole task. A closely related finding, the endowed progress effect, shows that giving people an artificial head start toward a goal (even with no change in the real remaining distance) accelerates completion, because motivation tracks *perceived* proximity to the goal, not just objective distance.
**Evidence:** Hull's original maze-running studies with rats found running speed increased as animals neared the goal box. Kivetz, Urminsky, and Zheng extended the effect to humans with field data from café loyalty-card programs and online grocery loyalty accounts, finding purchase frequency accelerated as customers approached their free reward. Nunes and Drèze's endowed-progress experiments showed that a 12-stamp loyalty card pre-stamped with 2 stamps (10 stamps remaining) drove faster, higher completion than a 10-stamp card starting at zero — identical objective distance, different perceived proximity, different behavior.
**Transfer to design:** Progress bars, completion percentages, and step counters ("3 of 5 steps done") work because they make proximity to a goal visible and construct a gradient the user feels pulled along. A legitimate, honest head start — crediting a user for profile fields the system already has, or starting a checklist partially complete because setup genuinely began — is good use of endowed progress: it reflects real progress, just framed to be legible.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The effect is exploitative, not legitimate, the moment the "progress" is fabricated rather than reflecting real completed work — padding a progress bar past what a user actually accomplished, or inventing arbitrary multi-step gates whose only purpose is to create gradient-chasing behavior toward a goal with no independent value to the user.
**Cite:** [GM-11], [GM-12], [GM-13]

## The Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina effect: open loops and resumption

**Field & mechanism:** Interrupted, unfinished tasks are recalled better and are more likely to be spontaneously resumed than completed ones — the mind appears to hold an "open loop" in a more active, accessible state until it is closed. Bluma Zeigarnik's original 1920s observation (from watching Berlin waiters recall unpaid orders more readily than paid ones) split into two related phenomena in the literature: the memory advantage for interrupted tasks (the Zeigarnik effect proper) and the tendency to spontaneously resume an interrupted task when given the chance (the Ovsiankina effect).
**Evidence:** Ghibellini and Meier's 2025 meta-analysis of the accumulated Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina literature finds the underlying effects are real but considerably smaller and more heterogeneous than the classic account suggests, moderated by how engaging the task was, the type and length of interruption, and individual differences — a useful corrective to the often-overstated "unfinished tasks nag at you" folk version of the finding.
**Transfer to design:** Leaving a task visibly, honestly incomplete (a draft saved but not submitted, a partially filled form, a paused download) can legitimately help users pick up where they left off — this is good state-preservation design, not manipulation, when the interruption was the user's own choice and the resumption serves their goal.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Manufacturing artificial incompleteness purely to exploit the resumption pull — fake unread-badge counts, streaks engineered to "break" for engagement rather than reflecting real activity, checklists with meaningless filler steps — uses the mechanism against the user's interest rather than in service of it, and the meta-analytic evidence for how strong and reliable that pull even is has weakened, making the ethical cost/benefit worse than gamification folklore assumes.
**Cite:** [GM-14]

## Loot boxes and gambling-adjacent monetization

**Field & mechanism:** Loot boxes — paid, randomized in-game rewards of variable value — combine variable-ratio reinforcement, near-miss-adjacent reveal mechanics, and real-money spending into a single system that structurally resembles gambling, even in games not legally classified as such.
**Evidence:** Zendle and Cairns' survey study found that money spent on loot boxes correlated with problem-gambling severity scores among video game players, controlling for general game spending — one of the first empirical links tying a specific, common monetization mechanic to gambling-adjacent harm, and a finding that has since fed into regulatory debate in multiple countries.
**Transfer to design:** Randomized-reward monetization is not automatically off-limits, but it carries a materially different risk profile than flat-price or skill-based unlocks, and that risk is concentrated among vulnerable users (including minors) regardless of a product's target audience. If a product includes randomized paid rewards, transparent odds disclosure, spend caps, and age gating are not optional nice-to-haves; they are the minimum response to a documented harm pathway.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The correlational survey design cannot establish that loot boxes *cause* problem gambling rather than problem-gambling-prone individuals being more drawn to loot boxes in the first place — treat the finding as a strong, actionable risk signal, not proof of a specific causal mechanism, and note that not all randomized-reward systems (e.g., a free, non-monetized surprise mechanic) carry the same risk profile as pay-to-randomize systems.
**Cite:** [GM-15]

## Dark patterns at scale

**Field & mechanism:** "Dark patterns" are interface designs deliberately crafted to steer users toward choices that benefit the business at the user's expense — through confusion, false urgency, obstruction, or emotional manipulation — rather than through genuinely better options. Several of the mechanisms above (fabricated progress, manufactured open loops, near-miss framing) are dark patterns when deployed with that intent.
**Evidence:** Mathur and colleagues' automated crawl of roughly 11,000 shopping websites found on the order of 1,800+ distinct dark-pattern instances across about a dozen categories (e.g., false countdown timers, confirmshaming, hidden subscription terms, forced continuity), present on a meaningful fraction of sites, with many instances traceable to a small number of shared third-party marketing scripts rather than bespoke design work — evidence that these patterns are often adopted off-the-shelf rather than invented per-product.
**Transfer to design:** Every mechanism in this file — reward schedules, progress framing, open-loop resumption, near-miss signaling — can be implemented in a form that serves the user (honest, informative, reversible) or a form that exploits them (deceptive, coercive, or engineered to produce anxiety/compulsion). Run new engagement features through a dark-pattern audit before shipping: would this design survive being fully disclosed to the user in plain language ("we show you a fake countdown to create urgency")?
**Where the analogy breaks:** The crawl methodology detects pattern *presence*, not always intent or actual user harm — some flagged patterns are genuinely ambiguous (a real, if aggressive, limited-time offer is not the same as a fabricated one) — so treat the taxonomy as a checklist for scrutiny, not an automatic verdict.
**Cite:** [GM-16]

## Ethics note

Every mechanism catalogued above sits on a spectrum from "engagement" to "compulsion," and the difference is not the mechanism itself but its honesty, its reversibility, and whose interest it serves. Flow and self-determination theory describe design that makes an activity *more genuinely rewarding* for the user — they are the ethical end of this spectrum, and there is no serious case against pursuing them. Variable-ratio reinforcement, engineered near-misses, fabricated progress, manufactured open loops, randomized paid rewards, and dark patterns are, in contrast, techniques for making a product harder to stop using regardless of whether continued use serves the user — several (loot boxes, near-miss mechanics, variable-ratio "pull to refresh" style loops) are drawn directly from gambling design and carry gambling-adjacent harm profiles, including for vulnerable populations and minors.

A working test: if you had to explain the mechanic to the user in plain language and it would embarrass you, or if the mechanic's primary function is to make disengagement harder rather than continued engagement more rewarding, it is out of bounds for this file's purposes — regardless of how well it performs on a retention dashboard. Compulsion loops that exploit reward-prediction-error mechanics, fabricated scarcity or progress, and randomized monetization aimed at extending spend are not "aggressive growth tactics" to be softened; they are the thing this file explicitly argues against building.

## Design checklist

- Match challenge to demonstrated skill and let difficulty ramp only as competence grows — scaffold with tutorials and progressive disclosure rather than a permanent easy mode or a punishing cliff.
- Design explicitly for autonomy (real choices with consequence), competence (specific, earned feedback on growing skill), and relatedness (genuine connection, not performative social features) rather than defaulting straight to points and badges.
- Prefer informational feedback ("you solved that twice as fast as last week") over controlling, reward-for-compliance framing, especially on tasks users already find interesting — the reward can crowd out the interest.
- Reserve unpredictable/variable-ratio reward framing for genuine delight (surprising discoveries, generous outcomes) and never for notification, refresh, or reveal mechanics engineered to maximize compulsive re-checking.
- Only show progress or proximity-to-goal information that reflects real, user-earned progress — an honest head start is good design; a padded or fabricated progress bar is a dark pattern.
- Let interruptions and incomplete states resolve on the user's terms; don't manufacture fake unread counts, artificially broken streaks, or meaningless filler steps purely to exploit the pull back into the app.
- If a feature includes randomized paid rewards, ship transparent odds disclosure, spend limits, and age gating alongside it — treat this as a legal-and-ethical floor, not a growth optimization to A/B test away.
- Before shipping any engagement mechanic, ask whether it would survive being described to the user in one honest sentence; if not, redesign it rather than obscure it.

## Deeper dive (v3)

## The PENS model: operationalizing SDT for games

**Field & mechanism:** The Player Experience of Need Satisfaction (PENS) model translates self-determination theory into a practitioner-usable framework specifically for games and interactive media, measuring in-game autonomy, competence, and (for multiplayer) relatedness satisfaction as direct predictors of enjoyment, preference, and continued play — independent of graphical fidelity or genre.
**Evidence:** Przybylski, Rigby, and Ryan's formal model paper found need satisfaction predicted game enjoyment, willingness to pay, and preference more strongly than surface features like violent content, and that it accounted for observed relationships between gameplay and short-term well-being or aggression outcomes that had previously been attributed directly to content. Rigby and Ryan's practitioner book extends the same framework into design guidance aimed at industry (a trade text rather than a peer-reviewed source, so treat its specific prescriptions as informed opinion built on the peer-reviewed PENS research, not as equivalent evidence).
**Transfer to design:** Use PENS as a lightweight audit lens for any interactive feature, not just games: does this feature give the user meaningful choice (autonomy), visible and growing capability (competence), and — where relevant — real connection to others (relatedness)? Features that fail all three are candidates for engagement mechanics propping up an otherwise unsatisfying experience.
**Where the analogy breaks:** PENS was developed and validated specifically on games; applying it to non-game software (a spreadsheet tool, an admin dashboard) is a reasonable heuristic transfer, not a validated one — treat PENS diagnostics on non-game products as a useful lens, not a measured result.
**Cite:** [GMX-01], [GMX-02]

## Effectance motivation: competence as a primary drive

**Field & mechanism:** Robert White's mid-century paper argued against the then-dominant drive-reduction model of motivation (that all motivation reduces to satisfying deficits like hunger or fear) by proposing effectance motivation: an intrinsic drive to interact competently and effectively with one's environment, satisfied by the process of mastering a challenge rather than by any external outcome.
**Evidence:** White's argument was primarily theoretical/synthetic — drawing on developmental observation (infants' persistent, undirected exploration and manipulation of objects with no external reward) to argue competence-seeking is a primary motive in its own right, not a byproduct of other drives. It predates and directly underlies the "competence" leg of self-determination theory decades later.
**Transfer to design:** Interfaces that let users visibly get better at using them — through practice, shortcuts, mastery of a tool's depth — tap a motivational source independent of any reward system layered on top; investing in genuine skill-growth affordances (keyboard shortcuts that reward practice, depth that rewards repeated use) pays a motivational dividend that badges cannot substitute for.
**Where the analogy breaks:** White's evidence base is developmental and observational by the standards of 1959, not a modern controlled experimental literature — treat effectance as a foundational, historically important concept that later, better-evidenced work (SDT, PENS) operationalized more rigorously, rather than citing White's paper itself as strong direct evidence for a specific design claim.
**Cite:** [GMX-03]

## The self-determination continuum and the performance nuance

**Field & mechanism:** Ryan and Deci's fuller SDT statement lays out a continuum of motivational regulation — from amotivation, through externally controlled and introjected (guilt/ego-driven) extrinsic motivation, to identified and integrated extrinsic motivation (the person has internalized the value of the activity), to fully intrinsic motivation — and argues that autonomy-supportive contexts move people rightward along this continuum, with well-being benefits at every step. A later 40-year meta-analysis by Cerasoli, Nicklin, and Ford adds an important performance-specific nuance to the plain "rewards undermine motivation" story.
**Evidence:** Ryan and Deci's continuum is supported by cross-domain evidence (education, health behavior, work motivation) that autonomy-supportive framing predicts better internalization and persistence than controlling framing, even for activities that are not inherently interesting. Cerasoli, Nicklin, and Ford's meta-analysis of four decades of performance studies found intrinsic motivation was the stronger predictor of performance *quality*, extrinsic incentives were the stronger predictor of performance *quantity*, and — the key nuance — when incentives were directly and transparently tied to performance itself (rather than to mere task engagement), they did not undermine the predictive power of intrinsic motivation the way the classic overjustification narrative would suggest.
**Transfer to design:** Frame required or extrinsically-incentivized activities in terms of their genuine value to the user ("this step protects your account," not just "complete this to unlock X") to support internalization along the continuum. Where incentives are used, tie them transparently to genuine performance or output rather than to mere participation, which the meta-analytic evidence suggests is the less corrosive design.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The continuum model is a useful map, not a measurement instrument — placing a specific user's motivation for a specific feature at a specific point on the continuum is an inference, not something the model measures directly; and the Cerasoli nuance applies to performance-contingent incentives specifically, not to the participation-contingent, non-transparent reward schedules (loot boxes, variable-ratio notifications) flagged elsewhere in this file.
**Cite:** [GMX-04], [GMX-05]

## Gamification outcomes: a meta-analytic reality check

**Field & mechanism:** "Gamification" — adding game elements (points, badges, leaderboards, levels, narrative) to non-game contexts, especially learning — is one of the most widely adopted and widely oversold applications of this whole literature; a meta-analysis specifically of learning contexts gives the clearest evidence-based read on what it actually delivers.
**Evidence:** Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis of gamified learning interventions found small-to-moderate positive effects on cognitive, motivational, and behavioral learning outcomes overall, but with substantial heterogeneity: effects depended heavily on *which* game elements were combined and how, with some combinations (e.g., badges plus narrative plus levels together) outperforming single-element additions (leaderboards alone), and some studies finding null or even negative effects.
**Transfer to design:** Treat "gamify it" as underspecified advice — the evidence supports thoughtfully combined, need-satisfying elements (tied back to autonomy/competence/relatedness) over slapping a leaderboard or badge system onto an existing flow and expecting a reliable lift. Pilot and measure; the meta-analytic effect size is modest and highly implementation-dependent, not a guaranteed win.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The meta-analysis is scoped to learning/educational contexts specifically; extrapolating its effect sizes to unrelated domains (enterprise software, e-commerce) is a plausible but unvalidated transfer, and "small-to-moderate positive effect on average" does not mean gamification is risk-free — see the dark-patterns and loot-box entries above for how the same toolkit misfires.
**Cite:** [GMX-06]

## Dark game patterns: exploitation built into the mechanics

**Field & mechanism:** Zagal, Björk, and Lewis extended the dark-patterns concept from e-commerce/UX specifically into game design, cataloging patterns where the game's *mechanics themselves* — not just its interface — are structured to exploit players: manufactured grinding to pressure pay-to-skip purchases, deliberately obscured odds, social-obligation mechanics that guilt players into spending or returning, and monetization designed around psychological pressure points rather than genuine value delivered.
**Evidence:** The paper is a conceptual/taxonomic contribution (a design-studies framework built from case analysis of existing games) rather than an experimental study — its evidentiary weight is in naming and categorizing a real, observable design phenomenon, not in effect sizes.
**Transfer to design:** Use the taxonomy as a pre-mortem checklist specifically for *mechanics*, not just UI copy: does a progression system's pacing exist to teach and reward skill, or to manufacture frustration that a purchase relieves? Is a social feature designed to genuinely connect users, or to weaponize social obligation as a retention lever? The mechanic can be a dark pattern even when every individual screen looks clean.
**Where the analogy breaks:** As a taxonomy rather than a measured-effects study, it tells you what to look for, not how much harm a given instance causes or how prevalent it is — pair it with the Mathur et al. dark-patterns evidence above (which does offer prevalence data, in an e-commerce rather than game context) when prioritizing which patterns to eliminate first.
**Cite:** [GMX-07]
