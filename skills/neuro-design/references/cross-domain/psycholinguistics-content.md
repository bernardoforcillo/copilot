# Psycholinguistics & content design -> design

Psycholinguistics studies how people process language in real time — parsing sentence structure, resolving what a phrase refers to, and integrating new information with what they already know, typically within a fraction of a second and under real working-memory limits. It matters for interface design because almost every piece of UX writing — microcopy, labels, error messages, confirmations, instructions — is language a user has to parse instantly, usually mid-task, distracted, or under some degree of stress: conditions much closer to the pressured, incidental comprehension studied in this field than to unhurried reading of a document written to be studied. The findings below describe measurable costs and failure modes in ordinary sentence comprehension. Good UX writing avoids many of them by instinct, but knowing the underlying mechanism turns a vague "this copy feels off" into a specific, fixable diagnosis.

## The given-new contract

**Field & mechanism:** Haviland & Clark proposed that communication runs on an implicit "given-new contract": a sentence is structured into a "given" part (information the speaker assumes the listener already has or can identify) and a "new" part (what's being added), and comprehension proceeds by searching memory for an antecedent matching the given information, then attaching the new information to it. When a direct antecedent exists, that search is fast; when it doesn't, the listener has to construct one through extra inferential "bridging," which measurably slows comprehension. Clark & Haviland elaborated the contract as a cooperative convention speakers and listeners tacitly rely on. Clifton & Frazier later tested the assumption at the heart of the contract — that given information must precede new — directly, and found the ordering preference is real but not absolute: it interacts with focus and contrastive structure rather than holding as a rigid rule.

**Evidence:** Haviland & Clark's sentence-reading-time experiments compared sentences with a direct antecedent ("We got some beer out of the trunk... The beer was warm") against otherwise-identical sentences requiring a bridging inference ("We checked the picnic supplies... The beer was warm"), finding the bridging versions took reliably longer to read even though the "new" content was the same. Clifton & Frazier's reading-time studies manipulated given/new order directly and found given-before-new is often but not universally preferred, with violations tolerated when the new-first order is properly marked.

**Transfer to design:** Structure instructional and confirmation copy so the given part — what the user already knows or just did — comes first and anchors to context already established, with the new part (what's changing, what to do next) attached to it: "Your file was uploaded. Now choose who can see it" rather than opening on the new information with nothing for the reader to attach it to. Error messages that reference an object or state the user has no active memory of ("Token expired") force exactly the bridging search the research measures; naming the referent explicitly ("Your session, started 2 hours ago, expired") removes it.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The given-new contract was studied with short, constructed lab sentences that had exactly one obvious candidate antecedent. Real UI copy often has several candidate referents on screen at once (multiple files, multiple form fields), so "put given information first" is necessary but not sufficient — ambiguous reference resolution among several candidates is a separate problem the contract itself doesn't solve.

**Cite:** [PL-01], [PL-02], [PL-03]

## The cost of negation

**Field & mechanism:** Clark & Chase's sentence-picture verification studies found that negative sentences ("The star isn't above the plus") consistently take longer to verify against a picture than affirmative sentences, with false negatives the slowest condition of all — evidence that the mind represents the affirmative core of a proposition first and applies negation as an additional processing step, rather than representing "not X" as a single native unit. Kaup and colleagues extended this: negation appears to briefly activate the very state being denied before that representation is suppressed and updated — reading "the door is not open" transiently activates "open" on the way to settling on "closed."

**Evidence:** Clark & Chase's reaction-time verification tasks produced the classic ordering — true affirmatives fastest, false negatives slowest — across many affirmative/negative and true/false combinations. Kaup, Lüdtke & Zwaan's reading-time and probe-verification experiments showed the negated concept remains transiently active even when it is ultimately rejected, evidence that negation is processed as an operation on an activated representation, not a clean logical inversion.

**Transfer to design:** State rules, settings, and permissions affirmatively wherever the underlying fact allows it: "Visible to team" reads faster than "Not hidden from team," and a toggle labeled around a negation ("Notifications off") invites the same extra processing step the lab studies measure, versus a plain state label ("Notifications: Off") that isn't phrased as a negated instruction. Double negatives in warnings ("Don't uncheck this unless...") stack the cost and should be rewritten as direct, affirmative instructions.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The lab tasks used simple, decontextualized geometric statements verified against an unambiguous picture. Real negation in UI copy is often doing real communicative work — denying a specific expectation ("No, we didn't charge your card") — where the negation is the point being made, not incidental phrasing; eliminating it isn't always the right fix, only unnecessary or stacked negation is.

**Cite:** [PL-04], [PL-05]

## Garden-path sentences and parsing

**Field & mechanism:** Frazier & Fodor's "sausage machine" model proposed that sentence parsing happens in two stages: an initial stage builds structure fast using simple, local heuristics such as minimal attachment (prefer the syntactically simplest analysis) within a small processing window, and a second stage checks that structure against fuller context, revising it if needed. This explains "garden-path" sentences, where the initial simple parse turns out wrong and has to be detectably reanalyzed — the extreme textbook case being "The horse raced past the barn fell." Frazier & Rayner's eye-tracking work showed this reanalysis directly in reading behavior: regressive eye movements and longer fixations at the point where the initial parse breaks down.

**Evidence:** Frazier & Fodor's analysis established minimal attachment and late closure as parsing heuristics that predict exactly where garden-path breakdowns occur in ambiguous sentences. Frazier & Rayner's eye-movement recordings during reading of such sentences showed measurable disruption — longer fixation durations and regressions back to earlier text — precisely at the disambiguating word, linking the parsing model to observable reading behavior rather than just accuracy scores.

**Transfer to design:** Avoid syntactically ambiguous phrasing in instructions and error text where a natural first reading turns out wrong and has to be revised mid-sentence — "Remove the file you uploaded yesterday from your shared library" can briefly parse as a delete instruction when it's only a description. Front-load the verb and its object, and avoid nested or nominalized clauses, especially in copy read under time pressure with no chance to reread carefully.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Garden-path effects are demonstrated with carefully engineered full sentences read in isolation; most UI microcopy (a 2–5 word label) is too short to garden-path in the classic sense. This principle applies mainly to longer instructional copy, tooltips, and error explanations — for short labels, word predictability and the given-new contract matter more.

**Cite:** [PL-06], [PL-07]

## Good-enough processing

**Field & mechanism:** Ferreira and colleagues' "good-enough" processing account argues that comprehension doesn't always compute a full, accurate representation of a sentence — readers frequently settle for a shallow, heuristic interpretation that's good enough for the immediate task, even when it's demonstrably wrong, and unlike a garden path, this shallow representation can persist uncorrected. It shows up most clearly with "noncanonical" sentences — passives and other non-default word orders — where readers fall back on a plausible-agent-first heuristic for who did what to whom, overriding what the actual syntax specifies.

**Evidence:** Ferreira, Bailey & Ferraro's review compiled evidence that comprehenders often fail to fully recompute meaning even after encountering information that should force a correction, retaining an initial, sometimes-wrong gist. Ferreira's own experiments on noncanonical sentences ("The dog was bitten by the man") found participants frequently misassigned thematic roles — reverting to the plausible-agent-first reading even though the passive syntax specifies the opposite — and that this misinterpretation wasn't reliably fixed by simply rereading.

**Transfer to design:** Assume users are extracting a shallow gist rather than fully parsing every UI sentence. This favors canonical, default word order (agent–verb–object: "You deleted the file," not "The file was deleted by you") in confirmations and instructions, since noncanonical structures are exactly where good-enough parsing misassigns who did what. It also argues against packing a single consequential fact into one dense sentence and assuming a wrong first read will be caught on reread — the research suggests it often isn't.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Good-enough processing was demonstrated under normal, unhurried lab reading conditions and still showed shallow processing — meaning it's a floor, not a worst case. Real UI reading under time pressure, small screens, or interruption should be expected to be shallower still, so the lab effect sizes are a conservative lower bound on what can go wrong in an interface, not a ceiling.

**Cite:** [PL-08], [PL-09]

## Readability formulas, and their limits

**Field & mechanism:** Flesch's readability formula scores text difficulty from two cheap, countable surface features — words per sentence and syllables per word — combined into a single number correlated with grade-level reading difficulty; it and its descendants remain the default automated "is this readable" check in software because they're trivial to compute, unlike genuine comprehension testing. Bailin & Grafstein's linguistic critique argues these formulas conflate surface countable features with the actual sources of difficulty — syntactic complexity, referential and cohesive structure, and conceptual difficulty relative to the reader's prior knowledge — none of which "words per sentence" measures directly: a long sentence can be easy if it's structurally simple and coordinate, and a short one can be hard if it's syntactically dense or assumes knowledge the reader lacks. DuBay's synthesis of the broader readability-research literature documents both the formulas' genuine predictive track record at scale and the persistent debate over what is actually driving that correlation.

**Evidence:** Flesch validated the formula against independently graded reading materials, establishing a real, replicable correlation between sentence/word length and measured difficulty across a large body of text. Bailin & Grafstein's analysis shows the formulas' predictive success is best explained as a correlational artifact — complex ideas tend to require longer sentences and words — rather than the counted surface features being what causally makes a given sentence hard. DuBay's synthesis, compiling decades of readability research, confirms the formulas' strong track record predicting aggregate comprehension outcomes while acknowledging they can't diagnose why any specific sentence is difficult.

**Transfer to design:** Use a readability score as a cheap smoke alarm, not a design target. A bad score is a useful signal that copy deserves a human look; mechanically shortening words and sentences to chase a numeric target, without fixing the actual cause (ambiguous reference, nested logic, unfamiliar jargon, assumed domain knowledge), can leave text that scores "easy" while remaining genuinely confusing, or produce choppier prose from artificial truncation. Fix the linguistic cause directly — the given-new, negation, parsing, and good-enough-processing sections above name the actual mechanisms a score can't see.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The formulas were built and validated on continuous prose (textbooks, general documents) read start to end. UI microcopy is often a single fragment read out of narrative sequence — a label, a tooltip — where "words per sentence" barely applies as a unit, and the formulas say nothing about the visual and interaction factors (proximity to the relevant control, timing, whether the user is under stress) that dominate real comprehension difficulty inside an interface.

**Cite:** [PL-10], [PL-11], [PL-12]

## Plain legal language

**Field & mechanism:** Charrow & Charrow applied psycholinguistic analysis directly to a real, high-stakes comprehension failure — standardized jury instructions — and showed jurors routinely misunderstand the legal instructions they are required to apply. They diagnosed specific linguistic culprits, not juror inattention: nominalizations (turning verbs into abstract nouns, "the making of a determination" instead of "deciding"), passive constructions that bury who's doing what, multiply embedded subordinate clauses, and misused or archaic technical terms.

**Evidence:** Charrow & Charrow tested comprehension of actual pattern jury instructions against paraphrased versions that removed those specific linguistic features while preserving the underlying legal content, and found comprehension improved substantially on the rewritten versions — isolating the linguistic form, not the legal concept itself, as the source of the original failure. The result became a founding empirical basis for the plain-language legal-writing movement.

**Transfer to design:** Terms of service, consent screens, and privacy notices are this same failure mode transplanted into software — nominalized, passive, multiply-embedded phrasing that satisfies legal review but that users demonstrably cannot parse. The remedy Charrow & Charrow demonstrated is specific and testable: replace nominalizations with plain verbs, name the actor, flatten embedded clauses, and give technical terms their plain meaning inline — then verify comprehension directly with users, the way the original study verified its rewrite, rather than assuming a simpler-sounding draft actually improved understanding.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Jury instructions are read (often heard) once, under a judge's authority, with no real opportunity to reread, highlight, or ask a clarifying question. Most consent and legal UI text can be reread, searched, and increasingly is paired with layered summaries or an "in plain English" toggle — remediation options a courtroom instruction doesn't have — so the ceiling on how much rewriting alone can fix is higher in software than in the original study's context.

**Cite:** [PL-13]

## The semantic (Moses) illusion

**Field & mechanism:** The Moses illusion is a robust finding that readers often fail to detect an obviously false or altered detail embedded in an otherwise plausible, familiar sentence: asked "How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?", most people answer "two," not noticing it was Noah, not Moses, who built the ark — because comprehension proceeds through partial, schematic semantic matching rather than verifying every word against stored knowledge. Van Oostendorp & de Mul showed the illusion is graded by semantic relatedness: the closer the substituted word is in meaning to the correct one (Moses and Noah are both biblical patriarchs), the more likely the distortion slips through undetected, because shallow semantic matching accepts a close-enough neighbor.

**Evidence:** Erickson & Mattson's original experiments systematically substituted single distorted words into well-known factual questions and measured detection failure, replicating the effect across many item sets; detection improved somewhat when participants were explicitly told to watch for errors, but errors still frequently went unnoticed, ruling out simple carelessness as the full explanation. Van Oostendorp & de Mul directly manipulated the semantic distance between the substituted and correct term and found detection failure rose as relatedness increased, isolating shallow semantic-similarity matching as the mechanism.

**Transfer to design:** Don't assume a user will catch an incorrect but plausible-sounding value sitting inside familiar, templated UI phrasing — a confirmation dialog reading "Delete your Downloads folder?" when the user actually selected Documents can go unnoticed precisely because "delete a folder" is the expected, schema-consistent frame, and the specific noun is exactly the part most likely to be skimmed past. Variable, consequential details in confirmation and irreversible-action copy — amounts, recipient names, file or folder names, dates — should be visually distinguished (bold, color, its own line) rather than embedded inline in an otherwise-formulaic sentence, since formulaic templates are exactly what invites this kind of shallow, schema-driven reading.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The illusion was demonstrated with general-knowledge trivia questions where the reader has no personal stake in catching the error. Consequential UI confirmations often do carry real stakes (a user's own file, their own money), which should improve vigilance relative to the lab studies — but the underlying mechanism, that schema-consistent distortions are hard to catch, doesn't disappear at higher stakes, it's only partly offset by them.

**Cite:** [PL-14], [PL-15]

## Word predictability in reading

**Field & mechanism:** Ehrlich & Rayner's eye-tracking studies established that words more predictable from preceding sentence context are fixated for less time, and skipped more often, during ordinary reading than low-predictability words — even controlling for word length and frequency — showing that continuous, moment-to-moment prediction from context is a normal part of fluent reading rather than an occasional strategy. Staub's review consolidates the effect's robustness: predictability speeds processing gradiently, with more-predictable words read faster along a continuum rather than in a binary predicted/unpredicted split, and this effect is real and separable from word frequency, a related but distinct source of ease.

**Evidence:** Ehrlich & Rayner's eye-movement recordings during sentence reading isolated predictability's effect on fixation duration and skipping rate from the confound of word length. Staub's synthesis reviews the accumulated eye-tracking literature, including studies using cloze-probability norms to quantify predictability directly, confirming the graded, continuous nature of the predictability benefit across many studies and reading paradigms.

**Transfer to design:** Where a word in a UI sentence is genuinely predictable from what precedes it — a confirmation button following "Are you sure you want to delete this file?" predictably ending in "Delete" — that predictability is a real processing asset: consistent, conventional phrasing reads faster because trained users can predict the next word or action from context. The flip side matters for safety: a highly predictable word in a high-stakes spot is also the word most likely to be skimmed past rather than actually read, which cuts against the goal in confirmations where you specifically want that word registered, not glossed over as expected filler.

**Where the analogy breaks:** These findings describe unconscious, low-level attentional allocation during silent reading of connected prose; they say nothing about whether a reader consciously understood or will remember what was read. A highly predictable, fast-skimmed sentence is exactly the kind a good-enough-processing reader is most likely to approve without registering its specific content, so predictability and comprehension safety can trade off against each other in a confirmation-dialog context rather than reinforcing one another.

**Cite:** [PL-16], [PL-17]

## Wording shapes memory, not just judgment

**Field & mechanism:** Loftus & Palmer's classic eyewitness-memory experiments showed that the specific verb used to ask about a witnessed event changes not just the immediate response but the underlying memory representation itself: participants asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other gave higher speed estimates than those asked about cars that "hit" each other — and a week later, the "smashed" group was also significantly more likely to falsely remember seeing broken glass that was never in the film at all. The wording wasn't just biasing a judgment at the moment of asking; it appears to have been folded into the stored memory trace itself, consistent with memory being reconstructive rather than a fixed recording.

**Evidence:** Loftus & Palmer's two-experiment design first established the verb-to-speed-estimate effect, then in a follow-up isolated genuine memory alteration — not just a biased response — by asking a new yes/no question about broken glass a week after viewing the film; the "smashed" group falsely recalled glass at a significantly higher rate than the "hit" group, despite no glass being present in either version.

**Transfer to design:** Wording choices in how you ask users to recall or report something — support intake forms, post-incident surveys, "what happened" bug reports — can shape not just how they answer in the moment but what they subsequently believe occurred; a leading verb ("How did the app crash?" versus "What happened before the app closed?") can distort a user's own account of an event they experienced. This sits beside a related but separate finding from decision research: describing identical facts as a gain versus a loss — the framing effect Tversky and Kahneman later demonstrated — shifts what choice a person makes, not their memory of a past event. Together the two lines of work make the same broader point from different angles: the specific words used to describe something are never a neutral wrapper around fixed underlying content, since wording itself becomes part of what's represented.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Loftus & Palmer's effect was demonstrated for episodic memory of a briefly witnessed, fast-moving, ambiguous event — exactly the conditions (brief exposure, no rehearsal, genuine perceptual ambiguity) under which memory is most reconstructive and suggestible. Most UI content isn't asking users to reconstruct a fuzzy memory of something they briefly witnessed, so applying this finding outside memory-report contexts — treating it as a general license for persuasive wording — overreaches what the study actually shows; it is specifically about contaminating recollection, not about the persuasive power of wording in general.

**Cite:** [PL-18]

## Design checklist

- Lead sentences with the given (what the user already knows or just did) before the new (what changed or what to do next); name an explicit referent rather than forcing a bridging-inference search.
- State rules, settings, and permissions affirmatively; avoid negated toggle labels and double negatives in warnings, which carry a measurable extra processing cost.
- Keep instructional and error copy syntactically simple with default agent–verb–object order; avoid nested clauses and noncanonical (passive) structures that invite shallow, misassigned "who did what" readings.
- Treat automated readability scores as a smoke alarm, not a target — diagnose and fix the actual linguistic cause of difficulty (ambiguity, embedding, jargon, assumed knowledge) rather than mechanically shortening words and sentences to hit a number.
- In legal and consent copy, replace nominalizations and passive constructions with plain verbs that name the actor, and verify comprehension directly with users rather than assuming a simpler-sounding rewrite worked.
- Visually distinguish critical variable details (amounts, filenames, recipients) in confirmation copy instead of burying them inline in a formulaic sentence template, which invites schema-driven reading that skims past exactly the part that matters.
- Use conventional, predictable phrasing for routine actions, but deliberately break the pattern for the one word that must actually be read and registered in a high-stakes confirmation.
- Word "what happened" prompts — support forms, incident reports, surveys — neutrally; leading verbs can distort not only a user's immediate answer but their later recollection of what occurred.
