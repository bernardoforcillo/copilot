# Trust, credibility & the uncanny valley -> design

Human-robot interaction and social-computing research spent five decades asking a question interface design now has to answer daily: when does a nonhuman agent earn a person's trust, and when does making it *seem* more human backfire? The uncanny valley names the perceptual trap (near-human appearance can repel rather than attract); trust-in-automation research names the behavioral trap (people systematically over- or under-rely on systems relative to how good those systems actually are); and the "computers are social actors" literature shows both traps are entangled with something deeper — people apply real social cognition to systems they know intellectually are not social agents. None of this was written with chatbots, AI copilots, or voice assistants in mind, but it describes exactly the design surface those products now occupy: an avatar's face, a chat bubble's typing indicator, a confidence score, a "trust me" tone of voice. This file imports the load-bearing findings on anthropomorphism, credibility heuristics, and calibrated reliance, and states where the transfer to AI/agent UI design holds and where it doesn't.

## The uncanny valley: Mori's hypothesis

**Field & mechanism:** Mori proposed that as a robot's or animated character's human-likeness increases, human affinity toward it rises — but not monotonically. Past a certain point, small remaining imperfections (skin that's almost but not quite right, motion that's almost but not quite fluid) produce a sharp plunge into revulsion and unease before affinity recovers and climbs again as likeness approaches indistinguishability from a real human. Mori additionally proposed that movement amplifies the effect: a still near-human object is less eerie than the same object animated, because motion recruits stronger, more specific expectations about how a living body should behave.

**Evidence:** Mori's original 1970 essay was a conceptual/theoretical proposal illustrated with a hand-drawn curve and examples (prosthetic hands, corpses, zombies, bunraku puppets), not a controlled experiment; the widely cited 2012 English translation is how most of the field encountered the hypothesis. It functioned for decades primarily as a design heuristic and a generative hypothesis for later empirical work.

**Transfer to design:** For AI avatars, virtual humans, and synthetic voices, treat "more realistic" as a design variable with a non-monotonic payoff curve, not a straightforward win. A stylized, clearly non-human avatar (a simple face, an abstract orb, a cartoonish character) can be safer and more trust-building than a near-photorealistic one that falls short in some detectable way — expression timing, eye contact, skin texture, voice prosody. If pursuing photorealism, budget for clearing the valley entirely (state-of-the-art facial animation, lip-sync, micro-expression) rather than landing partway.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Mori's hypothesis was built around physical robots and prosthetics with a single continuous "human-likeness" dial; software agents have many quasi-independent likeness dials at once (voice realism, avatar realism, conversational fluency, response latency, personality consistency) that can each sit at different points on the curve simultaneously, and it is not established that Mori's single-curve shape holds identically across all of them or that the dials interact additively.

**Cite:** [TR-01]

## Mapping the valley empirically

**Field & mechanism:** Mori's curve was a hypothesis, not a measurement. Mathur and Reichling built the first systematic empirical test using photographs of real robot faces spanning the human-likeness spectrum (rather than artists' renderings), having participants rate each face's human-likeness and eeriness, and — critically — measuring behavioral trust via an economic trust game where participants decided how much (real) money to entrust to each face.

**Evidence:** Both the likability and behavioral-trust measures traced a valley-shaped curve as a function of rated human-likeness: faces in the high-but-imperfect human-likeness range were rated most eerie and were trusted with less money in the economic game than either clearly-mechanical or clearly-human faces, confirming the uncanny valley is a real, dissociable dip in both subjective affect and downstream trust behavior, not just an impression from anecdote or artist sketches.

**Transfer to design:** This is the evidence that elevates the uncanny valley from folk design wisdom to an actionable constraint: the dip shows up in a behavioral trust measure, not just a "this looks creepy" rating, meaning it plausibly affects whether someone follows a recommendation or hands over sensitive information to a highly-but-imperfectly humanlike agent. When user-testing an AI avatar or synthetic spokesperson, measure downstream trust behavior (willingness to follow its advice, disclose information, complete a transaction) alongside subjective "does this feel off" ratings — the eeriness dip and the trust dip are correlated but were shown as two separate measurements.

## Why near-human triggers discomfort: the mind-perception account

**Field & mechanism:** Gray and Wegner proposed a specific psychological mechanism for uncanny-valley discomfort: perceived mind has (at least) two dimensions — *agency* (capacity to plan, act, and reason) and *experience* (capacity to feel, sense, and emote). Attributing agency to a machine is unremarkable and not unsettling; attributing experience to a machine — sensing that it can genuinely suffer, want, or feel — is what produces eeriness, because it violates a deep-seated boundary between mind-that-computes and mind-that-feels.

**Evidence:** Across experiments manipulating how entities (including robots) were described, entities framed as capable of experience (feeling pain, feeling afraid) were rated as eerier than the same entities framed only in terms of agency (capable of planning, remembering), isolating experience-attribution — not human-likeness or agency-attribution generally — as the specific trigger.

**Transfer to design:** This reframes what to avoid in AI/agent copy and behavior: claims or cues implying the system *feels* (a chatbot saying "I'm sad you're leaving," a voice assistant simulating hurt or excitement) are a more precise eeriness risk than appearance alone, especially when the system's actual competence obviously falls short of that emotional claim. Agency-framed language ("I found three matches and ranked them by relevance") reads as confident and useful; experience-framed language ("I really care about getting this right for you") is the higher-risk move into uncanny territory, particularly if the interface elsewhere signals the agent is clearly not sentient.

**Where the analogy breaks:** This is a highly specific, non-monotonic mind-perception account (only *experience* attribution is eerie, not *agency* attribution) that has not been demonstrated to be the exhaustive explanation for every uncanny-valley report — appearance-driven and motion-driven eeriness reports (Mori's original examples) don't always reduce cleanly to experience-attribution, so treat it as one well-supported contributing mechanism, not a full replacement for the appearance-based account.

**Cite:** [TR-03]

## Trust in automation & appropriate reliance

**Field & mechanism:** Lee and See define trust in automation as the attitude that an agent will help achieve a person's goals in a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability, and argue the design goal is not maximizing trust but *calibrating* it — matching trust to the automation's actual capability. Miscalibration in either direction is a failure mode: overtrust produces complacency and misuse (relying on automation past its actual competence), undertrust produces disuse (abandoning automation that is actually reliable). They identify three bases users draw on to calibrate trust: the system's demonstrated *performance*, the *process* by which it operates (how understandable its logic is), and its perceived *purpose* (why it was built, whose interests it serves).

**Evidence:** The framework synthesizes decades of human-automation research across process control, aviation, and decision-aid studies showing trust behaves like an attitude formed and updated through experience with a system, tracking measurable antecedents (reliability history, transparency, perceived intent) rather than being a fixed trait of either the user or the system.

**Transfer to design:** For AI features, this argues against optimizing purely for "make the user trust this more" and toward giving users accurate signal to calibrate against: show reliability history where relevant (this suggestion type is right X% of the time), make the process legible (why did it recommend this), and be transparent about purpose (is this optimized for the user's interest or the platform's). A companion research thread — Parasuraman's taxonomy of automation misuse, disuse, and abuse — describes the same over-/under-reliance dynamic from the operator-behavior side, and is worth knowing as background even though it isn't cited by key in this file.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Lee and See's framework was built primarily around single-purpose automation with a relatively stable, measurable competence level (an autopilot, a factory control system). General-purpose AI agents have competence that varies enormously by task and can't be summarized in one reliability number, which makes the "show a calibration signal" prescription harder to execute faithfully — a single accuracy percentage risks implying false precision about a system whose competence is actually task-dependent and hard to bound.

**Cite:** [TR-04]

## Computers as social actors: the media equation

**Field & mechanism:** Nass and colleagues' "Computers Are Social Actors" (CASA) research found that people apply social rules and heuristics to computers — politeness norms, reciprocity, gender stereotyping, in-group favoritism — even while explicitly denying, when asked directly, that a computer deserves such treatment. Reeves and Nass generalized this into the "media equation": people's responses to media (screens, voices, computers) are governed by the same social and natural rules evolved for responding to real people and places, applied largely automatically and unconsciously rather than as a deliberate choice to treat the machine "as if" it were a person.

**Evidence:** Nass, Steuer, and Tauber's foundational CHI studies showed users rated a computer that had "worked with them" more favorably (reciprocity), applied gender stereotypes to computer voices matching cultural stereotypes about that gender, and showed in-group bias toward computers labeled as being on their "team" — all while reporting, when surveyed, that treating a computer socially made no sense. Nass and Moon's follow-up review extended and replicated these findings across additional social categories (self-other distinction, applying politeness even to a computer that had just finished being evaluated), establishing the effect as mindless — triggered automatically by minimal social cues — rather than a considered belief that computers are social beings.

**Transfer to design:** Any interface with a conversational or first-person voice ("I noticed you..." / "Let's get started") is already invoking this machinery, whether or not it uses an avatar. Politeness norms, apology framing after errors, and reciprocity cues (the system "did something for you" before asking for input) will land with real social weight even on users who would deny that a chat interface deserves social treatment. This is leverageable for warmth and rapport, but it means tone, error-message phrasing, and even something as small as a greeting carry more behavioral weight in an AI product than the same words would in a purely transactional form.

**Where the analogy breaks:** CASA effects were demonstrated with minimal, largely incidental social cues (a text label, a synthesized voice) triggering surprisingly strong effects on 1990s-era interfaces with far less conversational sophistication than a modern AI agent — meaning today's much richer conversational cues could plausibly produce stronger effects than the original studies measured, an extrapolation the original research doesn't itself validate, and one that raises the ethical stakes of the design choice.

**Cite:** [TR-05], [TR-06], [TR-07]

## The MAIN model of online credibility

**Field & mechanism:** Sundar's MAIN model identifies four classes of technological affordance — Modality, Agency, Interactivity, Navigability — that act as *heuristic* cues people use to judge a source's credibility, often substituting for deep evaluation of the actual content. Modality cues (video vs. text, high production value) trigger a "realism heuristic." Agency cues (an identified author, an expert-system label, a bot disclosure) trigger "authority" or "machine" heuristics. Interactivity cues (comment counts, like counts, the ability to respond) trigger a "bandwagon heuristic." Navigability cues (site structure, ease of exploration) trigger a "browsing heuristic."

**Evidence:** Sundar synthesized experimental credibility research showing each affordance class shifts believability judgments independent of message quality — for example, source labels and interactivity indicators (like/share counts) measurably move perceived credibility even when the underlying content is unchanged, replicating the broader web-credibility finding (documented in Fogg's Stanford studies on what actually drives people's credibility judgments online, discussed in the social-influence file) that people rely heavily on surface and design cues rather than verifying content.

**Transfer to design:** For AI-generated content and agent responses, the same four levers apply directly: how content is presented (modality), whether and how authorship/agency is disclosed (a "generated by AI" label is itself an agency cue that shifts the heuristic used), whether the interface signals social validation (interactivity cues like upvotes on an answer), and how easy the surrounding interface is to navigate all shape perceived credibility independent of whether the AI's output is actually correct. This is a double-edged design lever: it can be used to build appropriately warranted trust (clear sourcing, visible confidence) or to inflate perceived credibility beyond actual accuracy (polished production values on a low-confidence answer) — a distinction that matters ethically, not just tactically.

**Where the analogy breaks:** MAIN was developed for a landscape of static and moderately interactive web content (news sites, blogs, early social media); generative AI systems that produce personalized, novel content on demand blur several of Sundar's affordance categories at once (an AI response is simultaneously highly "interactive" and has ambiguous "agency" — is the source the AI, its developer, or its training data?), so mapping a chatbot response cleanly onto one heuristic category is harder than it was for the web pages the model was built to describe.

**Cite:** [TR-08]

## Anthropomorphism: the three-factor theory

**Field & mechanism:** Epley, Waytz, and Cacioppo's theory explains anthropomorphism — attributing humanlike mental states to nonhuman agents — as driven by three factors that raise or lower the tendency: elicited agent knowledge (how easily human-based concepts come to mind as an explanatory framework for the agent's behavior, e.g., a humanlike face makes human concepts more accessible), effectance motivation (the desire to explain and predict an unpredictable agent's behavior — anthropomorphizing makes an otherwise-opaque agent feel more understandable and controllable), and sociality motivation (the desire for social connection, which rises when a person feels lonely or socially disconnected and makes them more likely to see minds in nonhuman agents).

**Evidence:** The theory synthesized and organized prior scattered anthropomorphism findings (pet ownership, attributing intent to gadgets, believing in supernatural agents) into a predictive framework, subsequently supported by experiments showing manipulating any of the three factors — increasing agent similarity to humans, increasing unpredictability, or inducing loneliness — independently increases anthropomorphism of a target.

**Transfer to design:** This predicts *when* users will be primed to anthropomorphize an AI product even without deliberate avatar or personality design: a system that behaves unpredictably (effectance motivation) or is used by someone in a socially isolated context (sociality motivation) will be anthropomorphized more readily than the interface's actual design choices alone would suggest. Designers should recognize that anthropomorphism is partly a property of the user's state and situation, not solely a dial the interface controls — which raises real questions about targeting lonely or vulnerable users with warm, personified AI framing.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The three-factor theory was built to explain everyday anthropomorphism broadly (weather, cars, deities, pets) and wasn't developed or validated specifically for conversational AI, where the "agent" can genuinely converse in humanlike language — a degree of elicited-agent-knowledge cue strength the original theory's examples (a sputtering car engine, a glitchy computer) didn't have to account for.

**Cite:** [TR-09]

## Anthropomorphism and trust resilience in automated agents

**Field & mechanism:** Two related findings show anthropomorphism doesn't just make agents likable — it changes how people trust them under stress and after failure. Waytz, Heafner, and Epley found that giving a simulated autonomous vehicle a name, gender, and voice (a minimal anthropomorphic treatment) increased trust in it, reduced how much blame participants assigned it after an unavoidable simulated accident, and made participants report feeling calmer, despite no change in the vehicle's actual driving behavior. De Visser and colleagues extended this to show anthropomorphized decision-aid agents display greater "trust resilience" — their users' trust declines less steeply after the agent makes an error than trust in an equivalent non-anthropomorphized agent.

**Evidence:** Waytz et al.'s driving-simulator experiment isolated the anthropomorphic treatment (name/voice/gender) as the only manipulated variable against an otherwise identical vehicle, directly attributing the trust and blame-attenuation shift to anthropomorphism itself. De Visser et al. ran decision-aid tasks with agents of varying anthropomorphism (from a machine-like interface through humanlike avatars to real humans) and tracked trust before and after the agent produced a wrong recommendation, finding trust dropped least for the anthropomorphized conditions.

**Transfer to design:** These findings mean anthropomorphic design choices for AI agents (a name, a voice, a persona) are not merely cosmetic — they measurably shift how much blame the system absorbs for its own errors and how forgivingly users respond to a mistake. That is a genuine trust-management benefit for products where graceful degradation matters, but it is also a lever that can be used to deflect appropriate scrutiny from a system's actual error rate, and should be applied deliberately rather than incidentally.

**Where the analogy breaks:** Both studies used relatively low-stakes, bounded errors (a simulated unavoidable accident, a wrong decision-aid suggestion in a lab task) — it is not established that anthropomorphism's trust-buffering effect holds, or is even desirable, for high-stakes real-world AI failures (a medical or financial AI's mistake), where absorbing less blame due to a friendly persona could work against, not for, user protection.

**Cite:** [TR-10], [TR-13]

## Algorithm aversion — and how to overcome it

**Field & mechanism:** Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey documented "algorithm aversion": people who watch an algorithm make even one small, explicable forecasting error subsequently trust it markedly less than they trust a human forecaster who made the identical error — and often switch to relying on the demonstrably worse human forecaster afterward, even though the algorithm outperforms the human on average. A follow-up study found this aversion is substantially reduced, and use of the algorithm restored, when people are given even minimal ability to adjust the algorithm's output themselves — a small, bounded degree of control over an otherwise-black-box system.

**Evidence:** The original studies used real forecasting tasks (predicting student performance, airline passenger volume) where participants saw either an algorithm or a human forecaster err, then chose which to rely on for further predictions; algorithmic error produced disproportionately larger subsequent avoidance than human error despite equal or better average algorithmic accuracy. The follow-up study experimentally varied whether participants could tweak the algorithm's forecast (even by a small, capped amount) and found this control restored willingness to use it to levels comparable to relying on themselves alone.

**Transfer to design:** AI features should expect a single visible error to cost disproportionate trust relative to the system's actual average accuracy — this is not irrational noise to be argued away but a well-replicated behavioral pattern to design around. The actionable mitigation is giving users a small amount of real adjustment control over AI outputs (edit a suggestion, weight a recommendation, override within limits) rather than presenting the AI as a fixed, unappealable black box; this measurably restores willingness to keep using the system after it errs.

**Where the analogy breaks:** The forecasting tasks used were repeated, quantifiable, low-personal-stakes prediction tasks with a clear right answer discoverable after the fact (actual student GPA, actual passenger count). Many AI product interactions (open-ended chat, creative generation, subjective recommendations) don't have that same clean, single-error-then-immediately-verifiable structure, so the sharp "one error triggers abandonment" dynamic may be less clean-cut, or may operate on subtler signals (a response that merely feels off), outside the study's controlled paradigm.

**Cite:** [TR-11], [TR-12]

## Overtrust of robots under real stakes

**Field & mechanism:** Robinette and colleagues tested whether people would follow a robot's guidance during a simulated building-emergency evacuation even after directly observing the robot perform badly. Participants first watched or experienced the robot behave erratically or lead them incorrectly in a non-emergency training run, then, during a staged smoke-filled emergency scenario, were guided by the same robot — including toward an exit that was visibly blocked or implausible.

**Evidence:** Participants followed the robot's guidance into the emergency scenario at high rates even after directly witnessing its prior unreliability, and even when its in-the-moment guidance visibly contradicted a viable path, demonstrating that authority-coded automation can retain compliance under acute stress despite concrete, recent, first-hand evidence that it is untrustworthy — a starker and more dangerous form of overtrust than the general automation-complacency literature typically documents.

**Transfer to design:** This is the sharpest cautionary finding in this file: under time pressure, stress, or an authority-coded interface (a robot, but by extension a confidently-styled AI agent presented as an authority), users may not perform even the error-correction they are objectively capable of and had just personally witnessed grounds for. High-stakes AI guidance systems (safety, medical, financial, emergency contexts) should be designed assuming users will over-comply with confident-sounding automated guidance under pressure, and should build in structural circuit-breakers (mandatory human confirmation, visible uncertainty, hard stops on clearly anomalous instructions) rather than relying on the user's own judgment as the safety backstop.

**Where the analogy breaks:** This was a physically embodied robot in a live simulated-emergency setting with genuine (if staged) urgency and physical presence, a context engineered specifically to maximize authority cues and stress; text-based or screen-based AI agents lack the physical co-presence and immediate bodily-danger urgency that likely amplified compliance here, so the extremity of the effect size shouldn't be assumed to transfer unchanged to a low-urgency chat interface — the qualitative risk (overtrust survives direct disconfirming evidence) is the transferable part, not the exact compliance rate.

**Cite:** [TR-14]

## Human trust in AI: an integrative view

**Field & mechanism:** Glikson and Woolley's review synthesizes the empirical literature on human trust in AI into a structured model distinguishing *cognitive trust* (a rational assessment of the AI's competence, reliability, and predictability, built from cues like tangibility, transparency, and immediacy of feedback) from *emotional trust* (an affective, relational response, shaped heavily by anthropomorphism and perceived warmth). The review maps how these two trust types develop differently depending on the AI's form of embodiment — physical robots, virtual/on-screen agents, and embedded, invisible algorithms each cue trust through a different mix of the two routes.

**Evidence:** Reviewing dozens of empirical studies across robotics, virtual agents, and algorithmic decision-aids, the authors find a consistent pattern: cognitive trust is built primarily through demonstrated reliability, transparency, and performance feedback, while emotional trust is built primarily through anthropomorphic and social cues (voice, appearance, personality) — and the two can diverge, producing agents that are emotionally trusted despite low actual reliability, or vice versa.

**Transfer to design:** Treat cognitive and emotional trust as two separate design targets that need separate evidence to justify: don't assume a warm, likable AI persona (emotional trust) substitutes for actual demonstrated reliability (cognitive trust), and don't assume a technically reliable but cold, opaque system will earn adoption without any emotional/relational design attention. Different embodiments (a voice assistant, an on-screen chat avatar, an invisible backend recommendation engine) should be expected to need different trust-building strategies, not one universal "trust playbook."

**Where the analogy breaks:** As a review, this synthesizes a heterogeneous literature spanning very different AI types (industrial robots, customer-service chatbots, hiring algorithms) with different stakes and interaction patterns; the cognitive/emotional trust distinction is a genuinely useful organizing frame, but the review itself notes the underlying primary studies vary widely in rigor and context, so specific numeric trust-antecedent weightings from any one embedded study shouldn't be treated as generalizing across all AI product categories.

**Cite:** [TR-15]

## Ethics note

Every mechanism in this file is dual-use. The same anthropomorphic cues that build appropriate warmth and rapport [TR-05], [TR-06], [TR-07] can be used to manufacture emotional trust [TR-15] that outruns a system's actual cognitive trustworthiness — a friendly voice or a named persona [TR-10] absorbing blame for real errors [TR-13] is a legitimate resilience benefit in a low-stakes context and a manipulation in a high-stakes one. The goal of applying this research to design is *calibrated* trust — trust that tracks the system's actual competence [TR-04] — not maximized trust. Concretely: do not use warmth, realism, or confident framing to paper over low actual accuracy; do not exploit the finding that a single visible error costs disproportionate trust [TR-11] by hiding errors or suppressing uncertainty rather than building genuine reliability or the small-adjustment controls that earn trust honestly [TR-12]; and take seriously that authority-coded automation can retain user compliance even after users have directly witnessed it fail [TR-14] — which places the burden of building in hard safety backstops on the designer, not on trusting users to self-correct under pressure. Disclosing that an agent is AI, disclosing confidence and limitations, and avoiding emotionally manipulative claims of experience or feeling [TR-03] the system does not have are not just good practice; they are what keeps anthropomorphic and credibility-heuristic design techniques on the "calibration" side of the line rather than the "manufactured overtrust" side.

## Design checklist

- Don't chase photorealism halfway: a stylized, clearly non-human avatar or voice is often safer than a near-human one that falls short on some detectable dimension (expression timing, prosody, texture).
- Avoid experience-claiming language ("I care," "I'm excited") for AI agents whose actual capability doesn't warrant it; agency-framed language ("I found and ranked these results") reads as competent without the eeriness or overclaim risk.
- Treat conversational tone, apology phrasing, and greetings as carrying real social weight even in a "just a chatbot" product — people apply social rules to it whether or not they'd endorse doing so if asked.
- Give users a real, bounded way to adjust or override AI outputs rather than presenting a take-it-or-leave-it black box; this measurably restores trust after a visible error.
- Disclose AI authorship/agency, show confidence or reliability signals, and make the reasoning process legible — build cognitive trust through evidence, not just emotional trust through persona.
- For high-stakes guidance (safety, medical, financial, emergency), assume users may over-comply with confident automated guidance under pressure and build in hard backstops — mandatory confirmation, visible uncertainty, anomaly stops — rather than relying on user skepticism as the safety net.
- Measure downstream trust behavior (willingness to follow advice, disclose data, complete a transaction), not just subjective likability ratings, when testing an AI persona or avatar.
- Expect a single visible mistake to cost more trust than its statistical weight — plan for graceful, low-friction recovery paths after AI errors rather than assuming average accuracy alone will sustain user confidence.
