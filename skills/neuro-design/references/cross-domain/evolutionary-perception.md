# Evolutionary perception & supernormal stimuli -> design

Evolution built the human visual and social-cognitive system to solve a narrow set of ancestral problems fast and automatically: find a face in the visual field before consciously deciding to look for one, read a threat in the grass before working out why it's alarming, and be captivated by a caretaking cue before reasoning about it at all. None of these circuits evolved to judge app icons, but interface design routinely — and often unknowingly — recruits them anyway. A face-shaped icon, a wide-eyed mascot, a round-cheeked avatar, an unpredictably-triggered notification badge: each borrows machinery that is millions of years older than the screen it appears on, and each can pull attention or emotion with a force disproportionate to the element's actual importance on the page. This file is the most ethically loaded of the cross-domain set for exactly that reason. Several of the mechanisms below describe not just what draws the eye, but what can be exaggerated to draw it harder than any naturally occurring version of the cue — a "supernormal stimulus" — and icon, mascot, avatar, and attention-cueing design is, whether the designer intends it or not, in the business of building things very much like them. Read the `## Ethics note` before applying any entry below.

## Face detection & the fusiform face area (Kanwisher et al., 1997)

**Field & mechanism:** Human visual cortex contains a region — the fusiform face area (FFA), identified via fMRI — that responds far more strongly to faces than to other object categories, providing a plausible neural substrate for fast, largely automatic face detection. A companion electrophysiological marker, the N170 (a negative EEG deflection peaking around 170ms after stimulus onset), shows the same face-selective sensitivity at far finer time resolution, indicating the response is not just anatomically localized but genuinely fast — faster than most deliberate, effortful perception. The machinery is functional from birth: newborns only hours old track a face-like arrangement of three high-contrast blobs (two "eyes" over one "mouth") further with their eyes and head than they track a scrambled control, evidence that some minimal face-detection template is present before visual learning could plausibly install it.
**Evidence:** Kanwisher, McDermott, & Chun (1997) used fMRI to show a fusiform region responding two to three times more strongly to face images than to assorted control objects, across tasks and in nearly all participants tested. Bentin et al. (1996) recorded ERPs showing the N170 was reliably larger and earlier for faces than for other complex stimuli, including hands, cars, and scrambled faces. Johnson et al. (1991) tracked newborn infants' eye and head movements toward a moving face-like schematic versus scrambled or blank controls, finding preferential tracking present within hours of birth.
**Transfer to design:** Any UI element with even a loose face-like configuration — two symmetric elements over one lower element, an icon, an app tile, a loading spinner with "eyes" — is detected and attended to faster and more automatically than an equivalent non-face shape, which is one reason mascots, avatars, and face-bearing icons reliably win the first fraction of a second of visual attention on a crowded screen; a small face-like avatar placed near a critical call-to-action or notification exploits real, involuntary attentional capture, not just aesthetic preference.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The "dedicated face module" account is not settled — Gauthier et al. (1999) showed the same fusiform region actively engages for non-face objects (novel "Greeble" shapes) once a viewer becomes a trained visual expert at individuating them, suggesting the region may implement a general expert-level, subordinate-category discrimination process that faces simply recruit most often (because everyone is a lifelong face expert), rather than a strictly face-specific module. Practically, the boundary predicts the effect generalizes weakly: a generic geometric logo does not get face-level attentional capture just by resembling a face loosely — the strongest capture is reserved for configurations close enough to a real face to engage the whole system, on either account of what that system is.
**Cite:** [EV-01], [EV-02], [EV-03], [EV-17]

## Face pareidolia (Liu et al., 2014)

**Field & mechanism:** Face pareidolia is the illusory perception of a face in an object that has no face — a burnt-toast "face," an electrical outlet's "expression," a car grille's "face." It is not merely a visual illusion but engages the same face-processing machinery used for real faces, at genuinely early processing stages, showing the face-detection system is tuned to a loose statistical template (roughly: two elements above one) rather than to strict face-identity verification.
**Evidence:** Liu et al. (2014) combined behavioral and fMRI evidence showing participants reliably "saw" faces in pure-noise images primed with a face expectation, and that this illusory face percept activated the fusiform face area much like a real face would, tracking subjective face-likeness ratings. Hadjikhani et al. (2009) used MEG — not EEG — to show the M170, the magnetic-field analog of the N170 timing marker, activated within roughly 165ms in response to purely object-based, face-like configurations (a percolator with two "eye" knobs and a "mouth" spout), confirming the face-detection response fires this fast even for objects that are obviously not faces once examined consciously.
**Transfer to design:** Any layout with two roughly symmetric elements positioned above one lower element — icon glyphs, a camera-and-speaker cutout, two controls above a slider — risks, or, used deliberately, benefits from, incidental face perception that draws attention and warmth the designer may not have planned for, or may want to court on purpose in a friendly, approachable interface.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Pareidolia is a byproduct of a low-specificity detector, not evidence the visual system treats these objects as faces in any full sense — people can usually distinguish a pareidolic face from a real one under scrutiny, and the felt "expression" is a fragile, top-down-assisted percept that survives only until closely inspected; leaning on it too literally, by anthropomorphizing every control on a screen, reads as gimmicky rather than warm once users look past the first glance.
**Cite:** [EV-04], [EV-05]

## Baby schema / Kindchenschema (Lorenz, 1943; Glocker et al., 2009)

**Field & mechanism:** Ethologist Konrad Lorenz proposed "Kindchenschema" (baby schema) — a cluster of infantile physical features (large head-to-body ratio, large eyes, round face, small nose and chin, chubby cheeks) — as an innate releasing mechanism that automatically triggers caretaking motivation and a "cute" response in adult observers, largely independent of the species or even the actual infancy of the bearer.
**Evidence:** Glocker et al. (2009) digitally manipulated the baby-schema intensity of infant face photographs (exaggerating or reducing head, eye, and chin ratios) and had adult raters judge cuteness and their own motivation to care for the child; higher baby-schema intensity produced higher cuteness ratings and stronger self-reported caretaking motivation in a dose-dependent way. A companion fMRI study in nulliparous women found baby-schema intensity parametrically modulated activity in the nucleus accumbens, a core reward-circuit structure, establishing the response as a genuine reward-system effect rather than just a self-report bias, present even without direct parenting experience.
**Transfer to design:** Rounded, oversized elements, generous eye-like accent shapes, and simplified, soft-edged mascot proportions reliably read as more approachable, trustworthy, and lovable — the design logic behind rounded app icons, chubby-proportioned mascots, and the near-universal "cute robot" aesthetic in consumer software, grounded in a real, dose-dependent, reward-circuit-level response rather than mere stylistic fashion.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The effect is a fast, low-level releaser triggered by proportions, not a judgment about the product's actual trustworthiness, safety, or quality — a baby-schema mascot can make an untrustworthy product feel warm, which is precisely the ethical hazard this file's Ethics note addresses. The effect also saturates and can tip into infantilizing or twee once overused in contexts — financial, medical, professional tools — where the user wants to feel respected as a competent adult rather than cared for as a dependent.
**Cite:** [EV-06], [EV-07], [EV-08]

## Supernormal stimuli (Tinbergen, 1951)

**Field & mechanism:** Nikolaas Tinbergen's classical ethology found that an animal responding to a fixed action pattern triggered by a specific stimulus feature — an "innate releasing mechanism" — can be induced to prefer an artificial, exaggerated version of that feature over the real, natural stimulus it evolved to track: a herring gull chick pecks harder at a thin red-striped rod than at its own parent's actual beak, and an oystercatcher will attempt to incubate an oversized dummy egg in preference to its own. Because the exaggerated stimulus overshoots anything occurring in nature, it is termed a "supernormal stimulus": it hijacks the releasing mechanism harder than any naturally occurring instance of the cue ever could.
**Evidence:** Tinbergen's controlled preference experiments presented animals — herring gulls, oystercatchers, stickleback fish, among others — with a natural stimulus alongside an artificial, feature-exaggerated alternative and measured which elicited the stronger instinctive response; in each case, the exaggerated artificial version won.
**Transfer to design:** The concept generalizes directly to any human perceptual preference built from an evolved feature-detector: a stimulus engineered to overshoot the naturally occurring range of that feature — color saturation beyond anything in nature, symmetry more perfect than any real face, a notification badge count that always climbs — can capture attention or trigger reward more strongly than the real-world cue the system evolved to track, which is exactly the mechanism underlying engineered engagement loops, hyper-saturated visual styling, and the exaggerated mascot proportions described in the baby-schema entry above.
**Where the analogy breaks:** Tinbergen's evidence is drawn from fixed action patterns in non-human animals with comparatively rigid, hardwired releasing mechanisms; human responses to supernormal cues are real but are moderated by conscious awareness, context, and higher cognition in ways a gull's pecking reflex is not — a person can notice a stimulus is engineered and discount it accordingly, so the effect is a real bias on attention and preference, not an inescapable behavioral lock the way it is for the animals in the original studies.
**Cite:** [EV-09], [EV-10]

## Evolved threat detection: snakes in the grass (Öhman et al., 2001)

**Field & mechanism:** Humans, like other primates, detect evolutionarily relevant fear-related stimuli — snakes and spiders, in the classic paradigms — faster than they detect equally novel but evolutionarily recent threats (guns, frayed wires) or non-threatening objects, consistent with a specialized, partly innate attentional bias favoring ancestral dangers over modern ones, regardless of which is statistically more dangerous today.
**Evidence:** Öhman, Flykt, & Esteves (2001) used visual search tasks embedding a single fear-relevant image among an array of neutral images, or vice versa, and found detection of the fear-relevant target was faster and less slowed by additional distractors than detection of a neutral target among fear-relevant distractors — consistent with privileged, largely pre-attentive "pop-out" detection of the ancestral threat category specifically. LoBue & DeLoache (2008) extended the paradigm to young children (as young as three) tested alongside adults, replicating faster snake detection in both age groups and arguing the effect appears too early in development to be explained solely by acquired fear learning.
**Transfer to design:** Threat- and warning-relevant visual features — angular, jagged edges, a coiled or serpentine silhouette, sudden high-contrast movement — draw attention faster and more automatically than equally salient neutral cues, one reason sharp, jagged iconography reads as more alarming than rounded shapes even before any learned association with warning colors; a lever worth using deliberately, and sparingly, for genuine error and destructive-action states.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The privileged detection is specific to a narrow, evolutionarily old category of ancestral threats (snakes, spiders, fearful faces); it does not generalize to the modern hazards a UI actually needs to flag — a malicious link, a phishing warning, a data-loss risk — which have no evolved detector at all and rely entirely on learned convention (color, iconography, copy). Don't assume any "urgent-looking" visual automatically confers the same evolved attentional priority the snake stimuli enjoy in the original studies.
**Cite:** [EV-11], [EV-12]

## Reflexive gaze cueing (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998)

**Field & mechanism:** Observing another person's eyes shift direction reflexively, and involuntarily, shifts the observer's own visual attention toward the same location, even when the gazing face provides no predictive information about where a subsequent target will appear and even when attending elsewhere would be more useful for the task.
**Evidence:** Friesen & Kingstone (1998) presented a schematic face whose gaze shifted left or right immediately before a target appeared at a spatially congruent or incongruent location; participants responded faster to targets at the gazed-at location even though gaze direction was non-predictive of where the target would actually appear, and even after participants were told the cue was meaningless — establishing the effect as reflexive rather than a deliberate, learned prediction strategy.
**Transfer to design:** A face, avatar, or illustrated character's gaze direction functions as a highly effective, largely involuntary attention-directing cue — a character looking toward a call-to-action, a key input field, or an important piece of copy reliably pulls the viewer's own gaze the same way, often more reliably than an arrow or other explicit directional graphic.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The effect is strongest for a genuinely face-like, eye-bearing stimulus at a scale and clarity where gaze direction is unambiguous — a small, stylized icon or a distant avatar may not carry a legible gaze direction at all, and the cueing effect does not transfer to non-ocular directional cues (a pointing hand, an arrow) with the same reflexive, sub-500ms speed, even though those cues can still work through slower, deliberate attention.
**Cite:** [EV-13]

## The uncanny valley (Mori, 1970/2012)

**Field & mechanism:** Robotics researcher Masahiro Mori proposed that as an artificial agent's human-likeness increases, observer affinity for it rises — up to a point at which near-perfect-but-imperfect human resemblance produces a sharp plunge into unease or revulsion (the "valley") before affinity recovers as resemblance becomes indistinguishable from an actual human. The original 1970 hypothesis remained speculative and largely untested for decades; the widely cited 2012 English translation of the original essay made it broadly available to an HCI and robotics audience, after which it accumulated substantially more, if still mixed, empirical attention.
**Evidence:** Mori's original piece is a theoretical proposal illustrated with a hypothesized affinity curve rather than a controlled experiment; it is included here specifically as a cross-domain link, because it names the same evolved face- and agent-detection machinery discussed throughout this file (the face-detection, pareidolia, and gaze-cueing entries above) from the opposite direction — instead of asking what draws attention toward a face-like stimulus, it asks what happens when a face-like stimulus is almost, but not quite, right. This same source is cited elsewhere in this skill's trust-and-credibility reference material under its own key, where the empirical follow-up studies on the effect's real shape and boundaries live.
**Transfer to design:** Avatars, mascots, and AI-agent representations benefit from committing clearly to one end of the human-likeness spectrum — deliberately stylized and abstract (a simple icon, a cartoon mascot), or, at real design and engineering cost, genuinely photorealistic — rather than landing in the ambiguous near-human middle, where small deviations (flat affect, mismatched proportions, almost-but-not-quite-natural motion) are most likely to read as unsettling.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The uncanny valley is a hypothesis with a mixed and contested empirical record — the size, shape, and even the existence of a true "valley" versus a simple monotonic affinity increase varies across studies, stimulus sets, and modalities (static images, animated motion, voice) — so it should be used as a directional design caution, not a precisely quantified curve to design against.
**Cite:** [EV-14]

## Ancestral priority in animal attention (New, Cosmides, & Tooby, 2007)

**Field & mechanism:** Human visual attention allocates privileged, faster processing to animals specifically, not just to any evolutionarily salient or expertise-relevant category — a pattern the authors argue reflects a dedicated ancestral-priority system tuned to animal detection broadly, a category that mattered for both predation risk and food acquisition across evolutionary history, rather than a generic "interesting or expert category" advantage.
**Evidence:** New, Cosmides, & Tooby (2007) used a change-detection paradigm — rapid alternation between near-identical scenes with one element changed — and found animals were detected significantly faster and more accurately than changes in people, vehicles, or plants, even after statistically controlling for modern-day familiarity, expertise, and threat-relevance, arguing against a purely learned-expertise or purely threat-detection account and for a category-general, ancestrally tuned "animate agent" detector.
**Transfer to design:** Any illustrated agent — a mascot, a pet-shaped icon, an animal avatar — plausibly draws faster incidental visual attention than an equally salient inanimate graphic of the same size and contrast, independent of whether the animal is cute, threatening, or neutral, worth knowing when choosing between an animal- and object-based icon or mascot for a feature that genuinely needs a first glance.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The advantage is measured in tightly controlled, briefly flashed laboratory change-detection displays; a busy, real-world interface with many competing salient elements, color, motion, and text will not reproduce a clean, isolated "animal advantage" the way a two-alternative lab display does, so the effect should inform icon and mascot choice at the margin, not be treated as a guaranteed attention-winner in a cluttered UI.
**Cite:** [EV-15]

## Biophilia (Wilson, 1984)

**Field & mechanism:** Biologist E. O. Wilson's "biophilia hypothesis" proposes that humans carry an evolved, partly innate affinity for other living systems and natural forms and processes — a preference for greenery, water, organic pattern, and living things — as a legacy of an evolutionary history spent almost entirely embedded in natural environments rather than built ones.
**Evidence:** Wilson's book is a synthesis and theoretical proposal rather than a single controlled experiment; it names and popularizes the broad evolutionary-preference-for-nature hypothesis that environmental-psychology researchers subsequently tested more directly. Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory and Ulrich's Stress Reduction Theory — covered in this skill's environmental-psychology and wayfinding reference material rather than in this file's own citation set — operationalize and empirically support pieces of the same underlying preference: natural content restores depleted directed attention and produces faster physiological stress recovery than built or urban content.
**Transfer to design:** Organic shapes, natural color palettes, greenery, and nature-derived imagery or texture plausibly tap a genuine, evolutionarily old preference rather than a purely cultural or trend-driven one, one reason biophilic accents in onboarding, calming, or stress-adjacent screens — health results, financial alerts — tend to test well, a lever worth pairing with the curved-contour preferences described in this skill's neuroaesthetics material.
**Where the analogy breaks:** The hypothesis describes a preference for, and psychological benefit from, real or richly rendered natural exposure sustained over real time — a view, a walk, a living plant — not a small decorative gesture. A leaf icon or a green accent swatch is a symbolic nod toward the effect, not a dose of it, and should not be assumed to carry anything like the restorative or preference-shifting power of the actual natural stimuli the hypothesis and its supporting evidence describe.
**Cite:** [EV-16]

## Ethics note

Two mechanisms in this file are, structurally, the same move: find a feature an evolved detector responds to, then push it past what occurs in nature. Baby-schema exaggeration and supernormal stimuli are not edge cases here — they are the file's organizing idea, and the same move that makes a mascot endearing or a notification hard to ignore is the move that makes a slot machine's near-miss compelling or a loot box's reveal animation grip harder than the actual odds justify. Face detection, pareidolia, gaze cueing, and ancestral animal attention are lower-stakes on their own, but they are frequently the *delivery mechanism* for an exaggerated cue — a face is what gets exaggerated into baby schema, gaze is what gets weaponized to drag attention toward a manipulative default, and a "cute" mascot's face is what makes a dark pattern feel friendly rather than adversarial.

The line this file draws is intent and proportion, not the mechanism itself. Using a warm mascot face, a well-aimed gaze cue, or a rounded, approachable icon to make a genuinely useful product easier to understand, more pleasant to use, or less stressful in a hard moment (a calm error state, a friendly empty state, a reassuring onboarding guide) is a legitimate use of machinery evolution happens to have given us. Using the same tools to manufacture urgency the product doesn't have, to make a predatory default feel safe, to dress up a dark pattern in a friendly mascot's face, or to engineer a reward cue that overshoots what the underlying action actually earned, is not delight — it is supernormal-stimulus exploitation of a detector the user cannot consciously opt out of. A working test: if the exaggeration is calibrated to make something true feel clearer or warmer, it belongs in this file's toolkit; if it is calibrated to make something false, risky, or unwanted feel acceptable, it does not, regardless of how well it performs on an engagement metric.

## Design checklist

- Use face-like configurations (icons, mascots, avatars) deliberately to win a genuine first-glance moment on critical actions — not on every element, or the effect saturates and the interface reads as cluttered with eyes.
- Calibrate mascot and icon proportions toward warmth and approachability with restraint; reserve strong baby-schema exaggeration (oversized eyes, rounded everything) for contexts that want to feel cared-for, and dial it back where users want to feel like competent adults, not dependents.
- Point illustrated characters' gaze toward what actually matters most on the screen — a call-to-action, key input, or important message — rather than at decorative or arbitrary space, since gaze pulls attention involuntarily whether or not that's the design intent.
- Commit avatars and AI-agent representations clearly to either simple/stylized or fully photorealistic; avoid the ambiguous near-human middle where small rendering flaws read as unsettling rather than charming.
- Reserve jagged, angular, threat-coded visual language for genuine error, warning, and destructive-action states; using it decoratively burns the cue's ability to signal real urgency when it's needed.
- Treat any engineered cue that exceeds its real-world counterpart — hyper-saturated color, a badge count with no ceiling, a reward animation bigger than the reward — as a supernormal stimulus and check it against the Ethics note's proportionality test before shipping.
- Use biophilic and organic-shape accents as a light, evidence-backed mood lever in stressful or calming moments, not as a substitute for actually reducing the underlying stress or complexity of the task.
- When a persuasive or attention-directing pattern relies on one of these evolved detectors, be able to state in one honest sentence what it's doing and why — if that sentence would make a user feel manipulated rather than helped, redesign it.
